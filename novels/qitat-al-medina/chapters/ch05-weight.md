Chapter 5: Weight


They did not own boots.

The first week was a scramble — borrowing, begging, trading favors. One pair of boots arrived from the French tourist, who had heard about the match through the medina's nervous system of gossip and appeared at the spice merchant's door with a Nike box. The box was a reddish-orange with the words PHANTOM ELITE.

"Pour le chat magique," he said.

The boots were too small. Youssef squeezed his toes into them. The cobblestones felt different now, solid underneath shoes.

They practiced on a small balding artificial turf in the heart of Hay Mohammadi, just off the busy lanes of Boulevard Ali Yaata. A neighborhood turf — Terrain de Proximité. The apartment buildings around it stood five stories high, their facades a faded white that had once been brighter, their windows latticed with metal bars, the laundry on the balconies their only flag.

The gardien was there. He was always there. When he crossed his arms, the back of his shirt wrinkled into a perfect Y. He didn't say much. He picked up his clipboard and nodded. He understood.

The surface was alien — flat, consistent, the ball running true and fast, in a way that felt dishonest after the cobblestones' constant argument and the beach's forgiveness. Passes arrived exactly where you aimed them. First touches didn't negotiate. The ground had no opinion.

"It's too easy," Isklander said and then overhit a pass that skipped off the turf at twice the speed he'd intended and struck the surrounding wall, green and white paint still wet: F'BLADI DELMOUNI.

"Not easy," Youssef said. "Different. Like the sand was different."

They learned. The way the turf pushed back against a planted foot. The way the ball skidded rather than rolled. The way a sprint on artificial grass felt like running on a drumskin — responsive, fast, the energy returning through the soles. Omar drew formations on a flattened cardboard box and propped it against the wall. They walked the patterns, then ran them, then played them at speed until the patterns dissolved into instinct and the instinct became movement. While the others drank from water bottles, Youssef licked the salt from his upper lip and watched Yazid run the lines. End to end. The boy never quit. Youssef glanced down at his boot — PHANTOM ELITE prominent again — he ran.

The gardien unfolded his arms and wiped his face with the back of his wrist. The corner of his mouth turned slightly upward toward the sun.

Two Fridays arrived hot and bright, the sky a blue so deep it looked solid. Word came around that Raja Club Athlétique had moved the match to their premier pitch, complete with an official referee, linesmen, a new clock and scoreboard.

The pitch was a stadium in miniature — bleachers, floodlights, nets white and taut. Raja arrived in a team bus. Their warm-up was choreographed, each player moving through the same sequence at the same time, their touches crisp and identical. Perfect.

The coach was a slight man, salt-and-pepper hair, green-and-white tracksuit. He walked the touchline with a limp he had earned long ago and long since forgiven. His hands circled in front of him. "Yallah, yallah," he called.

Youssef's team arrived on scooters and bicycles and a city bus that dropped off teammates, family, and friends from the medina. Their jerseys were counterfeit Morocco kits that had appeared through channels no one could name. Chams-Eddine's boots were two different sizes. Yazid had wrapped black electrical tape around his right shoe where the sole was separating from the upper. None of their shorts or socks matched.

The crowd surprised both sides. The story had traveled — medina and beach against the academy, cobblestones and sand versus artificial turf. Moroccan U14 National Champions versus an unknown. Families came. Old men who had once played on streets like the ones Youssef played on came. Teenagers with cell phones raised came. Another group showed up with surfboards in hand. It was Friday evening, and the crowd attracted more of a crowd.

Badr shook Youssef's hand at the center circle. His eyes went to Youssef's jammed toes.

"Ready?" Badr asked.

"Yes," Youssef replied.

Raja played with machine precision — each part in service to the next, each movement drilled and calibrated. Their passes ran on rails. Their pressing came in coordinated waves. The turf was theirs; they knew its speed, its bounce, the way the ball sat up or skidded. Their coach stood at the touchline, vocal and loud, pointing instructions with his index finger.

Youssef's team spent ten minutes chasing shadows.

The first goal came from a corner — Badr rising above Chams-Eddine, the header traveling a clean line into the back of the net. The second came five minutes before halftime, a loose pass from Omar intercepted and converted with two touches — trap, finish, the ball in the net before Bilal set his feet.

2-0.

Under the bleachers, in the thin shade, the silence sat in their mouths like a prickly pear — the small spines they had not seen, the sweet flesh further in than they could reach without bleeding. Chams-Eddine sat with his head between his knees. Isklander stared at his borrowed boots. The medina boys and the coastal boys sat apart, cobblestone and sand.

Youssef said nothing for a long time. He was thinking about Simo — about the gray cat sitting at the edge of the spice merchant's display, watching the pigeons settle, waiting for the gap that their comfort would create.

"We've been playing their game," he said finally. The words were quiet, directed at the ground. "We don't play their game." Youssef paused. "We play our mix."

He looked at Karim. Karim was already nodding.

The second half began, and the medina and the beach arrived together as one. Not the football they had tried to play in the first forty-five — careful, imitative, respectable — but the real thing. A blind exchange between Youssef and Omar at the touchline, the way they did in the medina. A long coastal stride from Karim folding into the medina's tight rotation as though it had always belonged.

Youssef received the ball in his own half with three defenders between him and the goal. His first touch was a nutmeg — the ball played to himself through the midfielder's legs, a move so casual it looked accidental. But his body was already turning as the ball went through, and by the time the defender understood, Youssef was two meters past him. The second defender committed to a challenge; Youssef dropped his shoulder left and went right, the ball glued to his instep. The third was a pass — weighted, angled, threaded through a gap that opened for a half-second as the defensive line shifted. Karim ran onto it, his finish clean, hard, into the upper right corner.

There was no celebration. 2-1.

The equalizer came ten minutes later. Isklander's speed, which in the medina's narrow streets was also a tool of survival, on this wide pitch was something the academy's right back had never seen — a motion that registered only as absence, the way Lalla crossed three meters of open ground. The cross was low and flat. Yazid met it with his instep — not a spectacular strike, but a precise one, the ball redirected into the net. Someone who had learned to score within spaces where there is no room for a backswing.

2-2.

The crowd was on its feet like the Derby. Old Hadj Brahim leaned forward on his cane and did not blink. The teenager's phone was steady. The two old men on the bench were on their feet. The scoreboard had become a question.
