Chapter 6: Sparrow


Four minutes remained.

Raja pressed forward, but their pressing had changed — it was hurried, reactive, and hurry creates space. A clearance came off a shin and bounced high just inside Youssef's own half. He did not flinch at the bounce. He adjusted, one step, never two, and the ball came down onto his instep as if it had been looking for him. The pitch opened ahead of him the way the medina never did — wide, green, without walls.

He ran. His cramped toes felt free again.

The first midfielder came at an angle and Youssef shifted the ball to his left foot without breaking stride, the way you step around a puddle, the challenge arriving at the place where the ball had been a half-second before. The second dropped into the passing lane. Ahead, the center-back was backing up, and Chams-Eddine was drifting into the empty green to his left — not running, not calling, watching the spaces between the players.

Youssef played it to him and kept going.

Chams-Eddine did not stop the ball. He touched it once, with the inside of his foot, and sent it back across the grass into the space Youssef was about to arrive in. It was the best pass anyone had made all night. When it left his foot, Youssef was still a step behind the center-back's shoulder. He saw the shoulder in front of him. He saw the white number on the shirt.

Then he was past it, and the ball was there, and the center-back was turning.

One more touch — the ball drawn back under his sole, the center-back's boot sliding through nothing — and there was only the goalkeeper.

The goalkeeper came off his line. He was tall and wore all black. He made himself large the way goalkeepers are taught.

Youssef held the ball until the keeper's knees bent, then lifted it.

Not struck — lifted. The way his mother placed bread on the cloth at dinner. A touch so soft it seemed to cost nothing, and the ball rose over the goalkeeper's outstretched gloves and fell into the net, the net moving the way a curtain moves when a window is opened — gentle, barely disturbed.

The sound that followed was the sound the Cafe La Rose De Medina made when Hakimi's penalty floated down the center three years before.

Two Raja defenders turned to the linesman with their arms up, the way defenders always do. And then the flag went up. Offside. The referee walked over to talk with the linesman. The call stood.

Youssef did not argue. Omar's mouth was open. Yazid's arms were wide. Chams-Eddine had both hands on his head. Youssef could not hear any of them. The crowd had gone somewhere. He could hear his own breath going in and out, and nothing behind it. The evening was hot, the turf still giving back the day, and his hands were cold.

Raja took the free kick fast, before the medina boys had turned around. One long ball. Luca had it at the halfway line and was running at the space their whole team had left behind.

Youssef ran after him. Not back to his place. After the ball.

"Youssef — hold!"

Omar's voice, somewhere to his left. He heard it the way you hear a radio in another apartment. He did not hold. He ran past Chams-Eddine, who was already where he should be, already turned, already in Luca's way, and Youssef went by him as if he were a post.

Luca slowed at the edge of the box, the ball under him, his head up, looking for Badr. Youssef came from the side. His leg went before he decided. His boot found Luca's ankle — not much, a brush, the kind of touch no one in the alley would ever call — and Luca went down as if the turf had been pulled out from under him. He rolled once, twice, a third time, holding his shin, his face turned toward the referee.

The whistle.

Just outside the box. Nobody from the medina said anything. They had all seen it. It was the only fair call of the night, and it was his.

They built a wall at the edge of the area, shoulder to shoulder. Youssef stood at the end of it.

Badr set the ball down himself. He did not look at the wall. He took four steps back and stood with his hands on his hips, breathing, the way a man waits for a bus. Luca, up now and not limping, said something to him. Badr didn't answer.

The whistle. Badr ran three steps and struck it. The ball went over the wall, bending away, and Bilal dove as if reaching across sand. His fingertips found it.

It went in anyway.

3-2.

The Raja faithful cheered. The quietness was louder.

There would be no extra time and the last minute wound down quickly, the medina pressing for the equalizer. The final seconds dissolved with the sounds of boots on turf.

Then the evening call to prayer rose from the mosque beyond the park. The muezzin's voice drifted over the floodlights, over the chain-link fence, settling across both pitches — the cracked concrete and the green turf — reaching all the way to Stade Mohammed V without distinguishing between them.

The final whistle came.

The medina boys gathered in a small ragged circle. Omar's hand stayed around Youssef's neck, pulling him close. Youssef had pulled the boots off. The turf burned, and he stood on it anyway.

From the bleachers, a voice said it once.

"Qit sihri."

Low, the way it had started on the concrete. Nobody joined in. Youssef looked up. For a moment there was a face, high in the stands, alone, brown and creased the way wind and salt leave a face. Then a family stood to go, and when they had passed, the place was empty.

The floodlights had moths in them now. Someone on the Raja side was coiling a hose.

In the bleachers, Hadj Brahim lifted his cane. They had lost, and the families applauded anyway.

The Raja players moved toward the green and white bus, talking among themselves, not looking back. Luca raised his arm and waved it like a flag — the offside call, again. He turned to Badr. Badr had not moved. Luca held there a moment, then ran to catch the others.

Badr found Youssef.

"That goal," Badr said, looking down at his own feet.

"Didn't count," Youssef replied.

"It counted," Badr said. "It counted," he repeated.

Youssef looked at the PHANTOM ELITE boots, tied together by their laces and hanging from his fingers.

"Coach wanted to know." Badr paused. He looked at Youssef's bare feet. "Where did you learn?"

"Rue de la Douane," Youssef said. He looked back toward the city. "Qitat al-Medina."

Badr did not answer immediately.

"Your father?" Badr asked.

"Benali," Youssef replied.

Badr glanced toward the touchline where the Coach had stood all afternoon. "Akesbi. He played with him."

Youssef looked up.

"The Sparrow," Badr said.

"The Sparrow," Youssef repeated.

"He knows you now."

Badr held the look for one breath longer than expected. Then he turned toward the green and white academy bus.

Hadj Brahim was coming down the bleachers one step at a time, the cane first. At the bottom he stopped and looked back — past the end of the stands, past the fence, toward the public park.

Youssef looked where he looked. The concrete pitch. The goals with no nets. Farther off, under the last lamp on the path, a man in a work cap stood with his hands at his sides. The cap was grey, the grey of cement dust. Then the man turned toward the road, and Youssef looked down at the boots in his hand.

Karim sat on the turf beside him and unlaced his cleats. He knocked them together once, the way you knock out sand, though there was no sand, and pulled his jellies on over his white socks. Then he stood and waited.

They walked toward the gate together, Karim's jellies printing soft shapes in the turf, Youssef's bare soles leaving nothing at all.
