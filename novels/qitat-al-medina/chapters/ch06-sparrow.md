Chapter 6: Sparrow


Four minutes remained.

Raja pressed forward, but their pressing had changed — it was hurried, reactive, and hurry creates space. Youssef collected a loose ball just inside his own half. He had felt the bounce before the bounce arrived — the way Hamza on the fountain wall had always felt the ball coming. Thirty plus meters from his own goal. Sixty from the other. The pitch opened ahead of him the way the medina never did — wide, green, without walls.

He ran. His cramped toes felt free again.

The first midfielder came at an angle and Youssef shifted the ball to his left foot without breaking stride, the way you step around a puddle, the defender's challenge arriving at the place where the ball had been a half-second before. The second defender dropped into the passing lane, but Youssef wasn't looking for a pass. He was looking at the space — the column of green between the center-back and the right back, a gap that would exist for two seconds at most before the defensive line corrected.

He pushed the ball forward with the inside of his right foot, a touch that sent it a few meters ahead and into the gap, and then he ran — through the green the way he ran through the medina when a game spilled from an alley into the wider street, the way Lalla moved when she decided to cross the open ground — and the ball was there when he arrived and the center-back was not.

The goalkeeper came off his line. He was tall and wore all black. He made himself large the way goalkeepers are taught.

Youssef lifted the ball.

Not struck — lifted. The way his mother placed bread on the cloth at dinner. A touch so soft it seemed to cost nothing, and the ball rose over the goalkeeper's outstretched gloves and fell into the net, the net moving the way a curtain moves when a window is opened — gentle, barely disturbed.

The sound that followed was the sound the Cafe La Rose De Medina made when Hakimi's penalty floated down the center three years before.

And then the flag went up. Offside. The referee walked over to talk with the linesman. The call stood.

Raja snatched the ball from the back of the net and set it down for the free-kick. The goalkeeper drove a long ball to Badr on the left flank. Badr trapped it cleanly, taking three quick, aggressive touches past midfield. The medina boys tracked back. Badr lifted his head and lofted the ball to the far post and Luca took it on his chest, dropped it to his foot, and drilled it low and hard. Bilal dove as if reaching across sand. His outstretched glove grazed nothing.

3-2.

The Raja faithful cheered. The quietness was louder.

There would be no extra time and the last minute wound down quickly, the medina pressing for the equalizer. The final seconds dissolved with the sounds of boots on turf.

Then the evening call to prayer rose from the mosque beyond the park. The muezzin's voice drifted over the floodlights, over the chain-link fence, settling across both pitches — the cracked concrete and the green turf — reaching all the way to Stade Mohammed V without distinguishing between them.

The final whistle came.

The medina boys gathered in a small ragged circle. Omar's hand stayed around Youssef's neck, pulling him close. Karim and the coastal boys had traded their cleats for jellies and white socks, soft shapes printing in the turf. Youssef had pulled the boots off. The turf burned, and he stood on it anyway.

In the bleachers, Hadj Brahim lifted his cane. They had lost, and the families applauded anyway.

The Raja players moved toward the green and white bus, talking among themselves, not looking back. Luca raised his arm and waved it like a flag — the offside call, again. He turned to Badr. Badr had not moved. Luca held there a moment, then ran to catch the others.

Badr found Youssef.

"That goal," Badr said, looking down at his own feet.

"Didn't count," Youssef replied.

"It counted," Badr said. "It counted," he repeated.

"I know," Youssef said. He smiled.

"Coach wanted to know." Badr paused. He looked at Youssef's bare feet, the PHANTOM ELITE boots tied together and hanging from his fingers. "Where did you learn?"

"Rue de la Douane," Youssef said. He looked back toward the city. "Qitat al-Medina."

Badr did not answer immediately.

"Your father?" Badr asked.

"Benali," Youssef replied.

Badr glanced toward the touchline where the Coach had stood all afternoon. "Akesbi. He played with him."

Youssef looked up.

"The Sparrow," Badr said.

"The Sparrow," Youssef repeated.

"He knows you now."

Badr held the look for one breath longer than expected. Then he turned toward the green and white academy bus.
