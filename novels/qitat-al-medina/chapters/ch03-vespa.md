Chapter 3: Vespa


His twelfth birthday began with msemen and honey, the flatbread folded and fried until the layers separated at the touch of a fork, and his father sitting across the low table in his work clothes, not yet gone, which meant he had arranged something with the foreman. The stadium could wait.

"Twelve," Ahmed said, and his hands rested on Youssef's shoulders — cement hands, the fingertips rough as dried mortar. "When I was twelve, I thought I was ready for everything."

"What happened?" Youssef asked.

His father's hands stayed on his shoulders and he looked briefly out through the small window in the corner of the kitchen. The white minaret of the Hassan II Mosque filled more of the window than the sky.

"Nothing happened. That was the problem."

Youssef did not know what to do with that. He picked at the msemen.

"Ain Diab," Youssef said. "Tomorrow. Khalid is taking us."

Ahmed's fingertips tightened — half a count — then let go. He looked back at the window.

"The water is colder than it looks," he said finally. "Even in summer."

The celebration was small. Fatima's tagine simmered. Neighbors in the courtyard. Aicha handed Youssef a crown made from cardboard and bottle caps that cast small bright circles on the walls. Aicha's eyes were Fatima's eyes, the same black.

In the shadows, Old Hadj Brahim, who had known Ahmed since they were boys, leaned in close. "The Phantom," he said. "Sent a ball through a gap between two parked cars. Barely wider than the ball itself. And it came back to his foot. Like it knew the way home."

Youssef listened. The story was a gift. It was also a weight.

The next morning, Youssef climbed onto the back of Khalid's blue Vespa behind Omar, and the three of them rode west out of the crowded medina, skimming past side mirrors, past the elbows of pedestrians, cats darting between moped wheels. Youssef stared up at the sky as the road bent toward the coast and onto Boulevard Sidi Mohammed Ben Abdallah.

The Atlantic first arrived not through his eyes but through his lungs — the salt hit the back of his throat before the blue hit his vision, his ribs expanded. And there it stood. The Hassan II Mosque. He had not been here since he was little. The memory was thin. The minaret rose two hundred and ten meters above him, a finger of bone pointing at the sky. Khalid and Omar continued talking about body surfing and football and jumping the waves, but Youssef could not hear.

His neck tilted back. The mosque listened.

Place Ain Diab. The beach. Families under umbrellas, the bright fabric snapping in the offshore wind. A row of camels resting near the boardwalk, their saddles bright with green and red wool, an old man in a black djellaba taking children for rides at twenty dirhams. Further down, horses for rent — slow, salt-stained, their riders too tall for them. Vendors selling fried sardines from steel carts, the oil hissing in the offshore wind. Children running at the edge of the surf, their shouts mixed with the call of gulls. And further down still, where the sand was packed hard enough to hold weight, a group of boys kicking a ball, no shoes, no shirts, no whistles, no rules.

Youssef stepped onto the sand and the ground changed its language.

Every first touch was softer. Every stride shorter. The ball didn't skip or argue — it stuck, and the sand absorbed the energy of his passes and returned something slower, something that had to be worked for. His calves burned in a place they had never burned before, deep in the muscles that governed balance, and for the first few minutes his body was a stranger to itself — the feet that knew cobblestones intimately did not yet know this ground.

But the sand forgave. He fell and the sand caught him. He reached for a ball and the ground received him. He tried a bicycle kick, and when he landed on his back the breath left him and then came back, the sand holding the shape of his body.

Karim was taller than the other coastal boys, thin as rope, and he moved through the sand without appearing to touch it. He played with the ocean's rhythm — no pause, each action flowing into the next without the stops and starts that the medina game demanded. Beside Karim was Younes — lean, dark-skinned, his curls bleached by sun and salt — the ball balanced on his foot like a thing he had practiced alone. He watched Karim's grace. He watched Youssef's hunger.

"You're from the old city," Karim said.

"How do you know?" Youssef replied.

"Your feet. Your movement, it's the cobblestones," Karim said.

Youssef looked down at his feet, bare, the soles cracked and hard from years of stone. "Maybe," he said.

"Out here the sand forgives. That's why we play different."

They played until the orange sun hung low over the water, and the game merged — medina precision passing into coastal flow, Youssef's tight turns opening into longer strides, Karim's sweeping runs folding into smaller spaces. Younes ran between them, finding the spaces neither of them had yet claimed. When the game ended, Omar and Khalid ran for the surf, salt already in their hair, the medina two hours behind them.

"There's a concrete pitch near here," Karim said when they stopped. "Real goals. You should come back. Bring your medina."
