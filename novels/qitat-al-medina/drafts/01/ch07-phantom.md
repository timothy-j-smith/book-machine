Chapter 7: Phantom


The ride home was all wind and the Vespa's engine rising in pitch and echoes as the streets narrowed around them. Youssef rode on the back. In the medina, the story was traveling — and something pressed against his ribs.

He climbed the stairs to the apartment. The kitchen was dim. In the back room, his father sat on the low bench, the cedar chest open before him, something red folded across his knees. The gold of an embroidered emblem caught the lamplight. Ahmed folded the cloth over it.

He looked up, saw his son in the doorway, closed the chest, and they walked out to the courtyard together.

His face was darkened by the sun. Something in his eyes had opened.

"They say you scored from thirty meters," Ahmed said.

"Maybe twenty-five," Youssef replied.

"They say the goalkeeper never touched it."

Youssef nodded. "The Phantom."

Ahmed reached. His hand stopped, the lime-stained fingers hanging an inch from cotton. Then it settled on Youssef's shoulder. The weight was different from the weight at the birthday table. Not heavier. Rearranged.

Youssef's ribs loosened.

"They broke ground on the mosque the year I was born." Ahmed tilted his head toward the sea. "I was seven when they set the last pillar." A pause. "That's what they called it then. Great Hope Watching Over Us."

Youssef nodded.

"I still see that last pillar going up." Ahmed closed his eyes. "But we cracked."

He opened his eyes. He looked at his son. Youssef tried to speak. Nothing came.

His father's shoulders had rounded over the years and Youssef hadn't noticed until now.

They both looked out through the narrow gap between buildings where the evening light fell in the medina — not directly but reflected off the opposite wall, arriving warm and secondhand, carrying the smell of cumin and cinnamon from the merchant's shop below.

In the echoes, Youssef could hear a ball on cobblestones. Other boys. Younger perhaps.

He held his father's hand for the first time in many years.

Above them, someone's laundry flapped in the night breeze. Beneath, red and white graffiti faded into the brick: MI ALMA VA A GANAR. Together they stood, fingers entangled, and listened to the sounds the streets made.

Below, in the doorway of the spice merchant's shop, Simo sat with his mismatched eyes half-closed, watching the younger boys play, his weight shifting from one paw to the other. Patient. Still. Reading the angles.

And into the night, the game went on.
