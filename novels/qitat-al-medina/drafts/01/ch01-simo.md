Chapter 1: Simo


The gray cat appeared at the mouth of the alley each morning before the muezzin's first call, its weight shifting from one paw to the other.

Youssef watched from the doorway of his family's apartment, barefoot, the tile cold beneath his heels. Below, the spice merchant was arranging his cones of cumin and paprika and ras el hanout, the colors already bright in the thin early light, and the cat — Simo, with his mismatched eyes, one copper and one gray — sat at the edge of the merchant's display and studied the pigeons that gathered near the fountain.

He never lunged. That was the first lesson.

Simo would sit, and the pigeons would settle, and then his weight would shift — not toward the birds but away from them, a quarter-turn that opened a line the pigeons hadn't thought to guard. When he moved, it was through the gap the birds had created by watching the wrong direction.

Youssef was eleven. He had been tracking the cats of Rue de la Douane for three years, watching them the way his father watched the evening news — elbows on knees, eyes narrowed, as if proximity to the screen might change what it was showing.

Hamza, the scarred orange tabby, kept his eyes half-closed and missed nothing. His ribs were pronounced against tight skin. He owned the plaza near the fountain and moved through the boys' football games with complete confidence. He did not flinch at the ball — he adjusted — always one step, never two, his body knowing where the ball would land before it arrived.

Lalla, the black female, kept to the doorways along Rue de la Douane and could remain perfectly still, eyes open, for over an hour and then cross three meters of open ground so fast that the motion registered only as absence — she was here, then she was there, and the space between held nothing the eye could catch.

But it was Simo, who was neither the fastest nor the strongest but who must have understood something the others did not: that the shortest distance between two points was not a line but a hesitation, a false step that opened the true path.

Over three years, their movements had entered his feet without permission. He changed direction now without planting, without telegraphing. He could feel a defender's weight shift as Hamza felt the ball's approach — not through seeing it but through the slight vibration in the air, the small disturbance that preceded the event. He could look in one direction while sending the ball in the opposite. The cobblestone had turned soft on the palms of his feet; they absorbed his landings, his jumps, his cuts. He could bend his toes around the ball, the way the cats cradled their litter.

"You move like one of them," Isklander had told him one afternoon, gesturing toward Hamza, who was watching their game from atop the fountain wall with a stillness that bordered on contempt.

In the apartment, Fatima set two glasses of mint tea on the low table and called his name. Youssef came inside and sat cross-legged on the cushion and drank. The sugar dissolving against his teeth, the steam carrying the scent into the small room where his sister Aicha still slept in the corner, her breathing even and undisturbed. Fatima brushed a piece of lint off Youssef's collar. Beyond the window, past the rooftops, the white minaret of the Hassan II Mosque rose against the Atlantic sky. No one in the family climbed the stairs to the roof to look at it.

"Your father left early," Fatima said. "Grand Stade."

Youssef nodded. His father's hands, when he came home in the evenings, carried cement dust in the creases of his knuckles and along the edges of his nails, and at dinner he held his bread carefully, as if his fingers were instruments he was still learning to use. But the men in the medina, the ones who sat outside the cafe and remembered, said that it was Ahmed Benali's feet and particularly his toes that had once done things with a football that the narrow streets still talked about. They called him The Phantom. The French papers had called him Le Fantôme, back then. Not because he was fast — others were faster — but because he was never where you expected, and by the time you understood where he'd gone, the ball was already past you.

No one talked about what happened after.

Youssef finished his tea and pulled on his Barcelona jersey — Messi on the back, the fabric faded to the color of the sky just before full dark, a gift from a French tourist who had stopped to watch a game two years before. Below, in the alley, Omar was already waiting, juggling the ball against the wall, effortlessly.

The cobblestones of Rue de la Douane were not kind. Every seam was a ridge that could send the ball skipping sideways, every drain cover bent the angle of a pass. The walls on either side stood close enough that a long pass had nowhere to go — wide enough for a moped, narrow enough that the only useful pass was a short one. The ground never forgot a rain — the stones held moisture in their joints for days, creating patches of slick ground that appeared and vanished with the weather.

The alley sloped toward the sea, toward the mosque. One team always played uphill. Ahmed taught Youssef one thing about football, only one: practice uphill.

This was the pitch. The walls were defenders. The drain covers were the bad bounces you had to read before they happened. The cats were the opponents who never committed the same way twice. And the cobblestones were the old teachers, the ones who punished a lazy touch by sending the ball into the spice merchant's display or off the wheel of a passing moped, and who rewarded a good one with nothing — the ball simply continued, and the absence of punishment was the only praise the stones offered.

Youssef stole the ball from Omar and began to move. Tap, tap, tap. He sliced the ball with his toes, creating a sharp backspin. The ball advanced a single meter into the dust, hesitated, and rolled back. He didn't look down. The ball was already at his feet, ready.

He did it again.
