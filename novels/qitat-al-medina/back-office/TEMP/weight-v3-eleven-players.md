Chapter 5: Weight


They did not own boots.

On the cobblestones nobody needed them. On the sand nobody wanted them. Now there was a pitch that was not theirs and two Fridays to get ready for it, and the medina went looking. Isklander came back from a cousin's with a pair that had been white once. Chams-Eddine came back with two boots that had never met each other, one black and one silver, the silver one a size bigger. He stuffed its toe with newspaper and said it was fine. Yazid had his own. The right sole was coming away from the rest of it, and when he walked it opened and closed like a mouth.

On the third morning the spice merchant called Youssef's name up the stairs.

The man at the door was taller than the doorway. Youssef knew him before he knew how. The hat. The sunburned neck. He stood with his weight back on his heels, as if the alley might be steeper than it looked. Two years ago he had stood at the top of Rue de la Douane and watched the game until the call to prayer, and then he had pulled a shirt out of his bag. Youssef was wearing that shirt now. Messi on the back, gone soft and pale from washing.

The man looked at the shirt. He smiled with one side of his face.

He held out a box, the same color as the paprika cones behind him. Black letters on the side.

PHANTOM ELITE.

"Pour le chat magique," he said.

Then he touched the brim of his hat and went down the alley toward the sea, and the crowd closed behind him the way water closes behind a stone. Simo watched him go from the edge of the display, one copper eye, one gray.

Youssef sat on the bottom stair with the box on his knees. The paper inside was thin and crackled. He put his face down to the boots. Not cumin. Not the sea. They smelled new, and nothing on Rue de la Douane was new.

He read the word on the side of the box. He read it again. He put the lid back on.

The boots were a little too small. He pushed his foot in and his toes hit the end and folded under. He squeezed them in anyway and pulled the laces tight. He stood. The cobblestones were down there somewhere, every ridge and seam of them, but he could not feel them anymore. The stones had gone quiet.

He walked to the fountain and back. His toes said no the whole way. He did not take the boots off.

Mornings belonged to Ain Diab. Evenings belonged to Hay Mohammadi.

In the mornings Khalid pressed the Vespa's back tire with his thumb before anyone climbed on, then pressed it again. Omar and Youssef rode with him, the bodyboard standing between Khalid's knees. The others came on the bus. By the time the sun was over the water they were all on the hard sand by the tide line, where the ground held weight, and Karim was already running.

Karim did not shout instructions. He ran, and you ran after him, along the waterline and back, until the medina boys' calves burned in the deep place the sand had found on the first day. Younes waited at the end of each run with the ball on his foot. He never let it touch the sand. Foot, knee, foot, the back of his neck, his heel, while he talked about something else. Isklander tried it once. The ball went into the sea and a little boy brought it back.

They marked the goals with piles of shirts. Bilal stood between them the way he stood on the concrete: upright, knees soft, ready to stop anything that came straight at him.

Karim did not shoot straight at him. He shot low and hard toward the far pile.

Bilal went.

Youssef saw the whole thing. Bilal's front foot pushed, and then there was nothing under him at all. He was flat in the air, arms long, his body longer than Youssef had ever seen it, the ball coming and his hands going out to meet it. He landed on his side and the sand went up around him. When it came down he was lying there with the ball against his chest.

He did not move. Sand in his eyebrows. Sand in his mouth.

Then he laughed. On his back, holding the ball up at the sky, he laughed until he coughed.

"Again," he said. "Karim. Again."

Up the beach, where the sand was dry and loose, Khalid sat with his bodyboard beside him, the leash coiled on top of it. Out past the break his friends sat on their boards, rising and falling, and one of them stood up in the water and shouted Khalid's name and waved both arms.

Khalid lifted one hand.

He stayed where he was.

At midday the Vespa carried them back into the heat, and the medina took them back too. Youssef's mother sent him for oranges. He went down past the spice merchant and the fountain and into the lane where the stalls pushed in from both sides, to Yazid's father's stall. Yazid was behind it, lifting crates onto a stack taller than he was. His arms shone wet. A crate went up, then another. His father weighed out the oranges, and Youssef held the bag open. Yazid saw him and lifted his chin. He did not put the crate down.

The bag was heavy. The plastic handles cut into his fingers all the way home, and he let them.

The turf at Hay Mohammadi sat in the middle of the apartment buildings, just off the busy lanes of Boulevard Ali Yaata. Terrain de Proximité, the sign said. The buildings stood five stories high on every side, their white gone the color of old teeth, their windows barred, the laundry on their balconies the only flags they had. Somebody's radio. Somebody's baby. Somebody's mother calling a name down into the dark.

The turf was beaten up twelve hours a day. Children in the afternoon, men after work, anybody with coins. In front of both goals it had worn bald, down to the black backing underneath. Little black crumbs of rubber lived in it. They got into socks. They got into boots. Youssef found them in his bed at night and in his ears in the morning.

The gardien sat by the gate at a small table with a metal box on it. He was there every evening. He was always there. The boys put their coins on the table in a pile, Omar counting, Isklander adding one more, Chams-Eddine turning out an empty pocket, and the gardien looked at the pile and then at them. He crossed his arms. When he turned to look at the pitch, the back of his shirt wrinkled into a perfect Y.

He did not say anything.

They had never played on anything so flat. The same everywhere. The ball ran true and fast and did not stop to think. On the stones every pass was an argument. On the sand every pass was a favor you had to ask for. Here the ground did not care. Passes arrived exactly where you sent them. Even the bad ones.

"It's too easy," Isklander said, and hit a pass to Omar that skipped off the turf at twice the speed he meant. It went past Omar, past the goal, and slapped into the wall at the far end, where someone had painted F'BLADI DELMOUNI. The paint was still wet. The ball came back with paint on it.

"Not easy," Youssef said. "Different."

They learned it by getting it wrong. The ball skidded instead of rolling. In sand a planted foot sank. On stone it caught. Here the turf pushed back up, and if you were not ready it pushed you over. Running on it was like running across the skin of a derbouka at a wedding. Every step came back up through the soles and asked for another.

Omar flattened a cardboard box from behind the spice merchant's and drew on it with a black marker. Circles for the medina. Crosses for the coast. Arrows that bent around each other. He propped it against the wet wall, and they stood in front of it with their hands on their hips. Then they walked it. Then they ran it. Then they played it fast, again and again, until the arrows went out of their heads and into their legs, and nobody looked at the cardboard anymore.

There were eleven of them. Omar counted every night when they came through the gate. Six from the medina. Karim and Younes and three coastal boys who called each other by names Youssef was still learning. Eleven. He counted again, and it was eleven again.

Yazid ran the lines. End to end, touch the line, turn, end to end. When the others stopped to drink, he kept going. When the others sat down, he kept going. He had wrapped his right boot in black electrical tape now, around and around where the sole was coming away, and it slapped the turf every step. Youssef licked the salt off his lip and watched him. Then he looked down at his own boots, at the word on them, and got up and ran beside him.

At ten o'clock the men who had booked the next hour did not come. The gardien looked at the dark gate. He looked at the boys. He did not get up to turn off the lights.

The next evening, when Omar put the coins on the table, the gardien slid them back across with two fingers. Omar left them there. The gardien slid them back again and went on looking at the pitch.

After that the lights stayed on. Every night. Ten o'clock, eleven. Moths came and went in the white. The laundry moved on the balconies. A man leaned out of a window in his vest and watched, and a woman came down in slippers to stand at the fence. The gardien sat by his box with his arms crossed and did not look at his watch.

On the ninth night Youssef pulled off the boots, and the nail on his big toe had gone dark, purple going to black. He pressed it. It hurt. He put the boots back on.

Two Fridays arrived hot and bright.

The sky was a blue so deep it looked like you could knock on it. They came on scooters, on bicycles, on the bus, the same as always, and walked through the public park in a bunch, past the bench, past the cracked concrete where Badr had pointed and said, "Not that." Youssef had stood at this fence with his fingers through the wire.

Today the gate in the fence was open. A man with a list read their names and stepped aside.

They walked through.

Nobody said anything. Behind him Youssef heard Bilal let out his breath.

There were four pitches, two by two, corner to corner. Three were for training. Small goals stood at their edges, and cones in rows, and a line of plastic men with no faces made a wall in front of one goal, waiting for a free kick that wasn't coming. The fourth pitch ran along the fence. That one was theirs today.

Youssef had seen pitches this good. On Khalid's phone, small enough to cover with his thumb. He had never seen one come out of the screen and lie down in front of him.

The turf went all the way to the edges and stopped at lines so white they looked wet. The nets did not move. Down one long side ran the fence, a few steps off the touchline, and through it the park. Down the other long side stood the stands, one bank of seats going up, a roof throwing a strip of shade across the top rows. A banner hung along the front rail: a painted trophy, a year, and the word CHAMPIONS. Behind the far goal a clock stood on a pole. It said 35:00.

He looked for what was wrong. On the near post, at the height of his hip, a chip of white paint was gone and the gray metal showed through. By one corner flag the turf was flattened and scuffed pale, where something heavy had been dragged. A seam ran across the far end, and where the two pieces met, one edge had lifted the width of a finger. In the third row of the stands one seat was cracked down the middle, and someone had taped it.

He liked the tape. He did not know why.

Through the fence, the concrete pitch looked small. Like something left out in the sun.

He stepped onto the turf.

His soles told him first. The ground came up too fast, too sure. Omar rolled him a ball to warm up, an easy one, the kind Omar had rolled him a thousand times, and it ran under his boot and kept going toward the fence. He chased it. Behind him Isklander knocked one to Chams-Eddine, and Chams-Eddine stepped on it and sat down.

They looked at each other. Eleven boys standing still on all that turf. Not running. Not anything.

Eleven. Six from the medina. Karim and Younes and the three coastal boys. Nobody else. They did not have a bench. They set a row of water bottles along the touchline on the fence side, because the other side was Raja's, and put down a plastic bag of oranges from Yazid's father's stall.

The Raja bus came through the main gate and stopped. The doors hissed. Eighteen boys stepped down one after another, in the same tracksuits, with the same bags on the same shoulders. Then a man carrying a big bag with a white cross on it. Then the coach.

He was a slight man, salt-and-pepper hair, a green-and-white tracksuit. When he walked, his left leg came forward a little late every step, as if it had to be asked. He walked anyway, up and down the touchline in front of the stands, his hands going round and round in front of him.

"Yallah, yallah."

Raja warmed up in two lines. Every player touched the ball at the same moment, turned at the same moment, ran at the same moment, the way the men in the mosque bend together in one long row. When it was done, seven of them went and sat on the bench under its little plastic roof with their jackets on. Beside the bench a man sat at a folding table with a laptop open in front of him.

The medina boys pulled on their shirts.

Red. Morocco red. A man from the souk had come up Rue de la Douane on the Tuesday with a plastic bag full of them and would not take any money. The red was a little too orange. The stitching wandered. The lions on the badge looked more like two of the medina's dogs. Nothing else matched. Omar's shorts were from school. Karim's were for swimming. Isklander's were too big, and he had tied them with a lace. Younes wore two different socks. Chams-Eddine had one black boot and one silver. Yazid's right boot was more tape than boot.

The referee looked at the red shirts. He looked at Raja's shirts. He looked at the red again. He walked over to the coach and said something and pointed, and the coach looked across at the medina boys for a long moment with his hands stopped. Then he clapped twice. Raja went back onto the bus.

When they came off again, they were all in white.

"Because of us," Omar said. "Because we only have one."

Nobody answered him.

The stands were filling, and that surprised everybody. Raja families at one end, little brothers with flags. And then the others, coming through the park and the open gate, more and more of them: families from the medina, grandmothers in djellabas, babies on hips. Old men who had played in the alleys before anybody's father was born, still arguing about it, climbing the steps one at a time. A crowd from Ain Diab with bodyboards under their arms and salt still white in their hair, who sat in a row and stood their boards up beside them. Khalid was with them. His board was dry.

Hadj Brahim sat in the middle of the stands with his cane between his knees and both hands on top of it. He looked at the pitch the way he looked at a rug somebody wanted to sell him.

Badr met Youssef in the center circle. Up close he was taller than he had been at the fence. He did not hurry anything, not his walk, not his hand. He shook Youssef's hand, and his eyes went down to Youssef's boots and stayed there a moment.

"Ready?" Badr asked.

"Yes," Youssef said.

The stands were on his left. The fence was on his right, and the water bottles, and the oranges. Behind him, at the clock end, Bilal stood in the goal. Ahead, at the end by the gate they had come in through, was the Raja goal.

The whistle.

The ball went away from him.

White shirt to white shirt. One touch, two. Pass. One touch. Pass. Youssef ran at it and it was gone. He turned and it was behind him.

He could see all of it. That was the strange part. He saw Badr's finger point. He saw Luca's mouth open to laugh. He saw the left back step up half a step before the ball went, and he saw where the ball would go before it went there, slow and clear, every shirt and every gap. It did not help. He saw it, and the turf carried the ball there faster than his legs could follow what his eyes had seen.

The press came again. White, then white, then white. He came up out of one and the next was already on him. On a turn the turf went out from under his boot. He put a hand down. Hot. Hot rubber in his nose. He was up before he knew he was up.

"Close him —"

"Who?"

"Him! Nine —"

"Where's —"

"Karim, wide, go wi—"

Nobody finished. The ball was already somewhere else.

Luca ran down the fence side at one of the coastal boys. The boy turned to go with him, and his feet went out from under him on the hot turf, and he sat down hard. Luca was gone. The boy got up and ran after him anyway, all the way back to their own box, and by the time he got there Luca had crossed it and Chams-Eddine had headed it out.

His toes hurt. His chest hurt. His tongue was dry and stuck to the top of his mouth. Ten minutes, and nobody in red had kept the ball longer than a breath.

The first goal came from a corner on the stands side, down at Bilal's end. Youssef saw it slowly. Chams-Eddine jumping, and jumping well. Badr starting his jump later and finishing it higher and staying up there, as if there were a step in the air only he could find. His forehead went through the ball. The ball went in a straight line into the top corner.

Chams-Eddine landed and stayed bent over with his hands on his knees. Raja did not celebrate much. They jogged back to the halfway line and waited.

The second came near halftime. Omar had the ball on the fence side, deep in their own half, by the corner of their own box. Youssef saw the trap before Omar did: three white shirts walking in, not running, close together. Omar turned to play it short, as he had a thousand times in the alley, off the wall to Youssef coming round. But there was no wall. The fence was right there, a few steps away, and the fence was not in the game. There was a white shirt where the wall should have been. It stepped in and took the ball off the end of Omar's pass. Clean. Polite.

Two touches. Shot.

Bilal was still standing the way he stood on the concrete, upright, waiting for the ball to come to him. It went past him low. The ball was in the net before Bilal set his feet.

2-0.

The clock behind Bilal ran down to zeros, and the referee blew.

At halftime they sat in the thin shade at the end of the stands.

The silence sat in their mouths like a prickly pear.

Chams-Eddine sat with his head between his knees. Isklander stared at his boots, the cousin's boots. The medina boys sat along the wall. The coast boys sat on the turf a little way off. Nobody had decided it. That was just where everybody had sat down.

Yazid opened the bag of oranges and passed them along without a word. Youssef dug his thumb into the peel. The orange was warm from the sun and so sour his jaw ached under his ears.

Up in the stands, near the end, a man sat with his arms crossed, and when he turned to look at the Raja bench, the back of his shirt wrinkled into a perfect Y.

Youssef thought about Simo.

Simo at the edge of the spice merchant's display. The pigeons coming down by the fountain, one, then three, then all of them. Pecking. Walking. Getting comfortable. Simo did not move. Simo waited. The pigeons got more comfortable, and when they were comfortable they spread out, and when they spread out there was a gap, and Simo was already in it.

Out on the pitch the Raja boys were drinking from bottles with their names on them and laughing at something Luca had said. Two of them were lying on their backs.

"We've been playing their game," Youssef said. He said it to the ground.

Omar looked up.

"We don't play their game." He stopped. He looked across at the coast boys. "We play our mix."

Karim was already nodding.

They changed ends. Now the gate was behind Bilal, and the clock stood over the Raja goal, in front of Youssef, back at 35:00.

The second half began, and the pitch got slower.

Not the ball. The ball was as fast as ever. But now Youssef could see it, and his legs could see it too, at the same time. On the stones it had always been that way. Everything moved like honey off a spoon. A white shirt drifted one step too far from another. Luca turned his head to say something. The left back put his hands on his hips.

Comfortable.

Youssef took the ball on the fence-side touchline, a few steps past halfway, and played it to Omar without looking, and Omar played it back without looking, and the two of them went up the line, give, take, give, with the white line beside them doing what the wall did in the alley. The defender between them turned left, then right, then stopped turning. Once Youssef showed him the start of a step inside, only his shoulder, and went on down the line, and the defender was still leaning into a step that never came.

Karim came from deep with that long coast stride and then shortened it, three quick steps, and folded himself into the small space between Omar and Youssef as if he had grown up on Rue de la Douane. The ball went through all three of them and came out the other side, at Karim's feet, at the edge of the Raja box.

The crowd made a low sound Youssef had not heard from them before.

Karim shot. It hit a white shin and went straight up.

After that it was not football. It was the alley. Bodies. Isklander went in with his head where a boot was coming. The keeper got both fists to the ball and punched it, and it hit somebody's back and dropped near the penalty spot with six of them falling around it. Youssef was on the ground with a white shirt across his legs. The ball was by his hand. He got a toe to it, not a kick, a push, sideways and blind, toward where Karim had been.

Karim was getting up. The ball hit his knee. It rolled. A white boot came across to clear it, a half-step late, and the ball crawled over the line and stopped just inside the goal, sitting there.

Karim did not celebrate. He walked in, picked the ball up, ran it back to the center circle, and put it down himself.

2-1.

On the stands side the coach's hands had stopped going round. He was walking faster now, and his left leg came later every step. "Yallah," he called, and it was a different yallah.

Raja came at them. The waves again, but now Youssef could see them coming. Then Luca was through. A ball over the top, one bounce, Luca alone at the edge of the medina box, nothing between him and Bilal but turf. Youssef was on the halfway line. He could not get there. He could only watch.

Luca hit it low toward the far post. A good shot.

Bilal went.

From the halfway line Youssef saw him leave the ground, the whole long length of him flat in the air above the turf. He had done this on the sand. Black crumbs flew up around him. His fingers found the ball and bent it, just enough, around the post.

He landed hard. The turf did not catch him the way the sand had. He lay there one second. Then he sat up, spat out a black crumb, and pointed at Chams-Eddine to take the near post.

He did not laugh this time.

The corner came in from the stands side. Chams-Eddine got his head to it in the six-yard box, and it went away high and spinning, out over the edge of the box toward the fence. Youssef's eyes were half-shut against the sun and missed nothing. He was not where the ball was. He was where it was going to be, already standing on the spot just outside the box when it came down, as if the ground had told him first. He took it on his chest and laid it off to Younes before the white shirts had finished looking up.

Younes was out by the fence-side touchline, short of halfway, with his back to the Raja goal and a defender breathing on his neck. The ball reached him too high, bouncing, about to go out against the fence. Anybody from the medina would have kicked it anywhere. On the stones a ball in the air is a ball you give away.

Younes did not kick it. He caught it between his heels, rolled it up the back of one calf with the other foot, and flicked it over his own head and over the defender's head, a slow arc against the sky, and it came down on the far side of the defender exactly where Younes had turned to meet it. It never touched the turf. In the bodyboard row somebody stood up and shouted his name.

Younes was over halfway now, facing the right way. He slid the ball down the line into the space in front of Isklander.

Raja's right back saw the ball. Then he looked for the red shirt, and there was only turf. Isklander had been beside him and now he was past him, and the space in between held nothing anybody could see. The right back was still turning when Isklander reached the corner of the box.

The cross came low and flat across the six-yard box, hard, skimming the turf, all the way to the far post.

Yazid was there. He had run the length of the pitch to be there. No backswing. He did not need one. He had learned to score in alleys where there was no room to swing a leg, where the wall was right there behind your heel. He put his instep to the ball and turned it, that was all, the way you turn the tap at the fountain, and it went in by the post.

2-2.

The stands came up all at once. The bodyboarders banged their boards together. Khalid held his over his head with both hands, and it was dry, and it didn't matter. In the middle of all of it Hadj Brahim leaned forward on his cane.

On the Raja bench the seven in jackets were standing.

Badr clapped his hands at his team, hard, three times. It was the first time Youssef had heard him raise his voice.

The clock over the Raja goal said 11:40.

Raja kicked off and did not stop. Three of the seven came off the bench and threw their jackets down on the turf. The coach was at the edge of the touchline now, almost on it. Long balls, short balls. A shot from outside the box that Bilal caught and lay on, holding it against his chest. The medina boys fell back without anybody telling them to. All eleven of them were in their own half. Then all eleven were around their own box.

Nobody said it. A draw. A draw against Raja was something you could carry home up Rue de la Douane.

The heat came up off the turf through the soles of his boots. His shirt stuck to his back. When the ball went out on the fence side, a medina boy walked to get it. When it went out on the stands side, a Raja boy ran.

9:58.

A Raja shot from the edge of the box hit one of the coastal boys in the stomach. He sat down. His mouth opened and no air went in. Karim pulled him up by the shirt, and the boy stood bent over, and then he went back to his place and stayed in it.

7:31. Badr took the ball himself and ran at them, and Chams-Eddine put his body in the way, and the ball went out off his hip by the corner flag on the fence side.

5:02. Youssef looked at the clock and wished he hadn't.

Yazid had not stopped running all game. Up to the Raja box for the equalizer, back to his own. Now he went again. A Raja ball over the top, down the fence side, and Yazid turned and sprinted back with it, past Omar, past the halfway line, and then he was not running.

He was on the turf with both hands around his right calf, face down.

Nobody had touched him.

The referee blew. The ball ran on and out by the fence, and nobody went to get it.

Youssef got there first. The calf was a hard knot. The knot moved under Yazid's skin by itself, a jump, then another. Yazid's teeth were together. His eyes were wet.

"It's —" Yazid said. "It's just —"

The others came. Omar. Bilal, running all the way from his goal. Karim, Isklander. They stood around him in a ring and looked down.

Youssef looked at the touchline.

Nobody. A row of water bottles, two of them fallen over. The plastic bag of oranges, half empty, the sun on it. The fence.

"His lace," Bilal said. "His lace is undone."

Nobody did it up.

On the stands side the coach had stopped walking. He turned to the folding table and lifted one hand, flat, and held it there. The man at the laptop touched something.

Youssef looked up at the clock over the Raja goal.

04:12.

He looked at Yazid. He looked back.

04:12.

Everything held. A Raja boy stood by the corner flag with his hands on his hips and did not move. The ball sat where it had stopped against the fence. Somewhere past the stands a moped went by, and it took a long time going. In the stands nobody sat down and nobody stood up.

The Raja man with the white-cross bag came first. He walked out from the bench across the whole width of the pitch, not hurrying, the bag knocking against his leg.

Then Youssef saw the other one.

He was coming down the steps of the stands, one at a time. He stepped over the rail at the bottom. He crossed the touchline beside the bench, and nobody on the bench said anything to him. He walked across the turf with his arms at his sides and went past the Raja man, who slowed, and stopped two steps away. He knelt beside Yazid. The back of his shirt wrinkled into a Y.

He took Yazid's heel in one hand and laid the leg out straight along the turf. He put his other hand flat against the bottom of Yazid's boot, against the tape, and leaned. Slowly. He pushed the toes back toward the shin, steady, with his weight behind it. Not hard. Not soft. His hands knew where to go. Youssef was close enough to smell them. Coins. The metal box.

Yazid made a noise in his throat.

The knot under his skin went still. Then it let go. Youssef watched the calf go soft again.

The Raja man stood with his bag. He did not kneel. He looked at the gardien's hands and nodded once, to nobody.

The referee came and stood over them, his whistle hanging on its string.

"Who are you?" he asked.

The gardien did not answer. He set Yazid's foot down on the turf the way you set down a full glass. He stood. He crossed his arms. Then he turned and walked back toward the stands, and he did not hurry, and nobody stopped him.

Yazid sat up. He pressed his calf with both thumbs. He stood, put his weight on the leg, lifted his heel, put it down.

"I'm okay," he said.

"Your lace," Bilal said.

Yazid bent and tied it. "I'm okay." He looked at Omar. Then at Youssef. "I'm okay."

Over the Raja goal the clock still said 04:12.

Youssef looked down at his boots, at the word on them. His toes were folded tight inside, the nail gone black.

He pressed them into the turf, and the turf pressed back.
