Chapter 5: Weight


They did not own boots.

On the cobblestones nobody needed them. On the sand nobody wanted them. Now there was a pitch that was not theirs and two Fridays to get ready for it, and the medina went looking. Isklander came back from a cousin's with a pair that had been white once. Chams-Eddine came back with two boots that had never met each other, one black and one silver, the silver one a size bigger. He stuffed its toe with newspaper and said it was fine. Yazid had his own. The right sole was coming away from the rest of it, and when he walked it opened and closed like a mouth.

On the third morning the spice merchant called Youssef's name up the stairs.

The man at the door was taller than the doorway. Youssef knew him before he knew how. The hat. The sunburned neck. The way he stood with his weight back on his heels, as if the alley might be steeper than it looked. Two years ago he had stood at the top of Rue de la Douane and watched the game until the call to prayer, and then he had pulled a shirt out of his bag. Youssef was wearing that shirt now. Messi on the back, gone soft and pale from washing.

The man looked at the shirt. He smiled with one side of his face.

He held out a box. Reddish-orange, the color of the paprika cones behind him. Black letters on the side.

PHANTOM ELITE.

"Pour le chat magique," he said.

Then he touched the brim of his hat and went down the alley toward the sea, and the crowd closed behind him the way water closes behind a stone. Simo watched him go from the edge of the display, one copper eye, one gray.

Youssef sat on the bottom stair with the box on his knees. The paper inside was thin and crackled. The boots smelled like nothing in the medina. Not cumin. Not bread. Not the sea. They smelled the way the inside of Khalid's phone looked.

He read the word on the side of the box. He read it again. He put the lid back on.

The boots were a little too small. He pushed his foot in and his toes hit the end and folded under. He squeezed them in anyway and pulled the laces tight. He stood. The cobblestones were down there somewhere, every ridge and seam of them, but he could not feel them anymore. The stones had gone quiet.

He walked to the fountain and back. His toes said no the whole way. He did not take the boots off.

Mornings belonged to Ain Diab. Evenings belonged to Hay Mohammadi.

In the mornings Khalid pressed the Vespa's back tire with his thumb before anyone climbed on, then pressed it again. Omar and Youssef rode with him, the bodyboard standing between Khalid's knees. The others came on the bus. By the time the sun was over the water they were all on the hard sand by the tide line, where the ground held weight, and Karim was already running.

Karim did not shout instructions. He ran, and you ran after him, along the waterline and back, until the medina boys' calves burned in the deep place the sand had found on the first day. Younes waited at the end of each run with the ball on his foot. He never let it touch the sand. Foot, knee, foot, the back of his neck, his heel, while he talked about something else. Isklander tried it once. The ball went into the sea and a little boy brought it back.

They marked the goals with piles of shirts. Bilal stood between them the way he stood on the concrete: upright, knees soft, ready to stop anything that came straight at him.

Karim did not shoot straight at him. He shot low and hard toward the far pile.

Bilal went.

Youssef saw the whole thing. Bilal's front foot pushed, and then there was nothing under him at all. He was flat in the air, arms long, his body longer than Youssef had ever seen it, the ball coming and his hands going out to meet it. He landed on his side and the sand went up around him like spray off a wave. When it came down he was lying there with the ball against his chest.

He did not move. Sand in his eyebrows. Sand in his mouth.

Then he laughed. On his back, holding the ball up at the sky, he laughed until he coughed.

"Again," he said. "Karim. Again."

Up the beach, where the sand was dry and loose, Khalid sat with his bodyboard beside him. The leash lay coiled on top of it like a sleeping cat's tail. Out past the break his friends sat on their boards, rising and falling, and one of them stood up in the water and shouted Khalid's name and waved both arms.

Khalid lifted one hand.

He stayed where he was.

At midday the Vespa carried them back into the heat, and the medina took them back too. Youssef's mother sent him for bread. He went down past the spice merchant and the fountain and into the lane where the stalls pushed in from both sides, and there was Yazid behind his father's stall, lifting crates of oranges onto a stack taller than he was. His arms shone wet. A crate went up, then another. He saw Youssef and lifted his chin. He did not put the crate down.

The bread was hot through the paper. Youssef held it against his chest all the way home. It burned him a little, and he let it.

The turf at Hay Mohammadi sat in the middle of the apartment buildings like a carpet laid down in a courtyard, just off the busy lanes of Boulevard Ali Yaata. Terrain de Proximité, the sign said. The buildings stood five stories high on every side, their white gone the color of old teeth, their windows barred, the laundry on their balconies the only flags they had. Somebody's radio. Somebody's baby. Somebody's mother calling a name down into the dark.

The turf was beaten up twelve hours a day. Children in the afternoon, men after work, anybody with coins. In front of both goals the green had worn away to the black underneath, the way the step at the spice merchant's door had worn into a dip from everyone who ever went in. Little black crumbs of rubber lived in the grass. They got into socks. They got into boots. Youssef found them in his bed at night and in his ears in the morning.

The gardien sat by the gate at a small table with a metal box on it. He was there every evening. He was always there. The boys put their coins on the table in a pile, Omar counting, Isklander adding one more, Chams-Eddine turning out an empty pocket, and the gardien looked at the pile and then at them. He crossed his arms. When he turned to look at the pitch, the back of his shirt wrinkled into a perfect Y.

He did not say anything.

The surface was not like anything they knew. Flat. The same everywhere. The ball ran true and fast and did not stop to think. On the stones every pass was an argument. On the sand every pass was a favor you had to ask for. Here the ground did not care. Passes arrived exactly where you sent them. Even the bad ones.

"It's too easy," Isklander said, and hit a pass to Omar that skipped off the turf at twice the speed he meant. It went past Omar, past the goal, and slapped into the wall at the far end, where someone had painted in green and white: F'BLADI DELMOUNI. The paint was still wet. The ball came back with green on it.

"Not easy," Youssef said. "Different."

They learned it the way you learn a new alley, by getting it wrong. The ball skidded instead of rolling. A planted foot did not sink like in sand or catch like on stone; the turf pushed back up, and if you were not ready it pushed you over. Running on it was like running across the skin of a derbouka at a wedding. Every step came back up through the soles and asked for another.

Omar flattened a cardboard box from behind the spice merchant's and drew on it with a black marker. Circles for the medina. Crosses for the coast. Arrows that bent around each other. He propped it against the wet wall, and they stood in front of it with their hands on their hips. Then they walked it. Then they ran it. Then they played it fast, again and again, until the arrows went out of their heads and into their legs, and nobody looked at the cardboard anymore.

Yazid ran the lines. End to end, touch the line, turn, end to end. When the others stopped to drink, he kept going. When the others sat down, he kept going. He had wrapped his right boot in black electrical tape now, around and around where the sole was coming away, and it slapped the turf every step. Youssef licked the salt off his lip and watched him. Then he looked down at his own boots, at the word on them, and got up and ran beside him.

At ten o'clock the men who had booked the next hour did not come. The gardien looked at the dark gate. He looked at the boys. He did not get up to turn off the lights.

The next evening, when Omar put the coins on the table, the gardien slid them back across with two fingers. Omar left them there. The gardien slid them back again and went on looking at the pitch.

After that the lights stayed on. Every night. Ten o'clock, eleven. Moths came and went in the white. The laundry moved on the balconies. A man leaned out of a window in his vest and watched, and a woman came down in slippers to stand at the fence. The gardien sat by his box with his arms crossed and did not look at his watch.

On the ninth night Youssef pulled off the boots, and the nail on his big toe had gone dark, purple going to black. He pressed it. It hurt. He put the boots back on.

Two Fridays arrived hot and bright.

The sky was a blue so deep it looked like you could knock on it. They came the way they always came, on scooters, on bicycles, on the bus, and walked through the public park in a bunch, past the bench, past the cracked concrete where Badr had pointed and said, "Not that." Youssef had stood at this fence with his fingers through the wire.

Today the gate in the fence was open. A man with a list read their names and stepped aside.

They walked through.

Nobody said anything. Behind him Youssef heard Bilal let out his breath.

There were four pitches, two by two, laid corner to corner like rugs in Hadj Brahim's shop. Three were for training. Small goals stood at their edges, and cones in rows, and a line of plastic men with no faces made a wall in front of one goal, waiting for a free kick that wasn't coming. The fourth pitch was the one beside the fence. That one was theirs today.

Youssef had seen pitches like this. On Khalid's phone, small enough to cover with his thumb. He had never seen one come out of the screen and lie down in front of him.

The green went all the way to the edges and stopped at lines so white they looked wet. The nets did not move. Along one side stood the stands, one long bank of seats going up, green and white, a roof throwing a strip of shade across the top rows. A clock at the end showed zeros. Every blade of grass stood straight up, as if nobody had ever stepped on it.

He looked for what was wrong. On the near post, at the height of his hip, a chip of white paint was gone and the gray metal showed through. In one corner a sprinkler head sat in the grass like a bottle cap. By the touchline there was a rectangle of green a little brighter than the rest, with a seam around it, where somebody had cut the old grass out and put new in. In the third row of the stands one seat was cracked down the middle, and someone had taped it.

He liked the tape. He did not know why.

Through the fence, the concrete pitch looked small. Like something left out in the sun.

He stepped onto the turf.

His soles told him first. The ground came up too fast, too sure, a ground with no questions in it. Omar rolled him a ball to warm up, an easy one, the kind Omar had rolled him a thousand times, and it ran under his boot as if it were late for something and kept going toward the fence. He chased it. Behind him Isklander knocked one to Chams-Eddine, and Chams-Eddine stepped on it and sat down.

They looked at each other. Eleven boys standing still in all that green, like cats caught in a moped's light at night in the alley. Not running. Not anything.

Eleven. That was what they had. Youssef, Omar, Isklander, Chams-Eddine, Yazid, Bilal. Karim and Younes and three coastal boys who called each other by names Youssef was still learning. Nobody else. They did not have a bench. They had a row of water bottles along the touchline and a plastic bag of oranges from Yazid's father's stall.

The Raja bus came through the main gate, green and white, and stopped. The doors hissed. Eighteen boys stepped down one after another, in the same tracksuits, with the same bags on the same shoulders. Then a man carrying a big bag with a white cross on it. Then the coach.

He was a slight man, salt-and-pepper hair, a green-and-white tracksuit. When he walked, his left leg came forward a little late every step, as if it had to be asked. He walked anyway, up and down the touchline on the club side, his hands going round and round in front of him.

"Yallah, yallah."

Raja warmed up in two lines. Every player touched the ball at the same moment, turned at the same moment, ran at the same moment, the way the men in the mosque bend together in one long row. When it was done, seven of them went and sat on the bench under its little plastic roof with their jackets on.

The medina boys pulled on their shirts.

Red. Morocco red. A man from the souk had come up Rue de la Douane on the Tuesday with a plastic bag full of them and would not take any money. The red was a little too orange. The stitching wandered. The lions on the badge looked more like two of the medina's dogs. Nothing else matched. Omar's shorts were blue. Karim's were white. Isklander's were black and too big, and he had tied them with a lace. Younes wore one white sock and one gray. Chams-Eddine had one black boot and one silver. Yazid's right boot was more tape than boot.

The referee looked at the red shirts. He looked at Raja, in their dark green. He walked over to the coach and said something and pointed, and the coach looked across at the medina boys for a long moment with his hands stopped. Then he clapped twice. Raja went back onto the bus.

When they came off again, they were all in white.

"Because of us," Omar said. "Because we only have one."

Nobody answered him.

The stands were filling, and that surprised everybody. Raja families in green at one end, little brothers with flags. And then the others, coming through the park and the open gate the way the tide comes up the beach: families from the medina, grandmothers in djellabas, babies on hips. Old men who had played in alleys like Rue de la Douane before anybody's father was born, still arguing about it, climbing the steps one at a time. A crowd from Ain Diab with bodyboards under their arms and salt still white in their hair, who sat in a row and stood their boards up beside them like a fence of their own. Khalid was with them. His board was dry.

Hadj Brahim sat in the middle of the stands with his cane between his knees and both hands on top of it. He looked at the pitch the way he looked at a rug somebody wanted to sell him.

Badr met Youssef in the center circle. Up close he was taller than he had been at the fence. He did not hurry anything, not his walk, not his hand. He shook Youssef's hand, and his eyes went down to the toes folded tight inside the too-small boots, and stayed there a moment.

"Ready?" Badr asked.

"Yes," Youssef said.

The whistle.

The ball went away from him.

It went from white shirt to white shirt like the tram down Boulevard Mohammed V, on rails, on time, never stopping where you were waiting. One touch, two. Pass. One touch. Pass. Youssef ran at it and it was gone. He turned and it was behind him.

He could see all of it. That was the strange part. He saw Badr's finger point. He saw Luca's mouth open to laugh. He saw the left back step up half a step before the ball went, and he saw where the ball would go before it went there, slow and clear, every shirt and every gap. It did not help. He saw it, and the turf carried the ball there faster than his legs could follow what his eyes had seen.

The pressing came in waves. White, then white, then white. You came up out of one and the next was already on you, the way it is at Ain Diab when you stand too long in the wrong place and the sea knocks you down and knocks you down again before you have your breath. On a turn the ground went out from under his boot. He put a hand down. The turf was hot. It smelled like the tire man's shop at the bottom of the hill.

"Close him —"

"Who?"

"Him! Nine —"

"Where's —"

"Karim, wide, go wi—"

Nobody finished. The ball was already somewhere else.

His toes hurt. His chest hurt. His tongue was dry and stuck to the top of his mouth. Ten minutes, and they had touched the ball the way you touch a pigeon: for half a second, before it goes.

The first goal came from a corner. Youssef saw it slowly. Chams-Eddine jumping, and jumping well. Badr starting his jump later and finishing it higher and staying up there, as if there were a step in the air only he could find. His forehead went through the ball. The ball went in a clean line into the top of the net, and the net that never moved, moved.

Chams-Eddine landed and stayed bent over with his hands on his knees. Raja did not celebrate much. They jogged back as if it had been the plan.

The second came near halftime. Omar had the ball by the touchline, and Youssef saw the trap before Omar did: three white shirts walking in, not running, close together, like a door swinging shut. Omar turned to play it short, the way he had a thousand times in the alley, off the wall to Youssef coming round. But there was no wall. There was a white shirt where the wall should have been. It stepped in and took the ball off the end of Omar's pass, clean and polite, like taking a glass of tea off a tray.

Two touches. Trap. Shot.

Bilal was still standing the way he stood on the concrete, upright, waiting for the ball to come to him. It went past him low. The ball was in the net before Bilal set his feet.

2-0.

At halftime they sat in the thin shade at the end of the stands.

The silence sat in their mouths like a prickly pear.

Chams-Eddine sat with his head between his knees. Isklander stared at his boots, the cousin's boots, as if they had done it. The medina boys sat along the wall. The coast boys sat on the grass a little way off. Nobody had decided it. That was just where everybody had sat down.

Up in the stands, near the end, a man sat with his arms crossed, and when he turned to look at the Raja bench, the back of his shirt wrinkled into a perfect Y.

Youssef thought about Simo.

Simo at the edge of the spice merchant's display. The pigeons coming down by the fountain, one, then three, then all of them. Pecking. Walking. Getting comfortable. Simo did not move. Simo waited. The pigeons got more comfortable, and when they were comfortable they spread out, and when they spread out there was a gap, and Simo was already in it.

Out on the pitch the Raja boys were drinking from bottles with their names on them and laughing at something Luca had said. Two of them were lying on their backs.

"We've been playing their game," Youssef said. He said it to the ground.

Omar looked up.

"We don't play their game." He stopped. He looked across at the coast boys. "We play our mix."

Karim was already nodding.

The second half began, and the pitch got slower.

Not the ball. The ball was as fast as ever. But now Youssef could see it, and his legs could see it too, at the same time, the way it was on the stones. Everything moved like honey off a spoon. A white shirt drifted one step too far from another. Luca turned his head to say something. The left back put his hands on his hips.

Comfortable.

Youssef took the ball near the touchline and played it to Omar without looking, and Omar played it back without looking, and the two of them went up the line like that, give, take, give, with the white line beside them doing what the wall did in the alley. The defender between them turned left, then right, then stopped turning. Karim came from deep with that long coast stride, the one that never seemed to touch the ground, and then shortened it, three quick steps, and folded himself into the small space between Omar and Youssef as if he had grown up on Rue de la Douane. The ball went through all three of them and came out the other side.

The crowd made a sound Youssef had not heard from them before. Low. Like when the whole café leans forward.

He got the ball in his own half with three white shirts between him and the goal.

The first came at him square. Youssef put the ball through his legs, a little push with the inside of his foot, so lazy it looked like a mistake, and his body was already turning while the ball was still between the boy's ankles. By the time the defender turned, Youssef was two meters gone.

The second waited for him, low, arms out. Youssef showed him the left. Not a step. The start of a step. Just his shoulder dropping, his weight going, the way you lean toward a door you are not going to walk through. The defender went with it. Youssef went right. The ball came with him as if it were tied to his instep.

The third was the back line, four white shirts in a row, and they slid across all together to close him, and in the sliding, for half a second, a gap opened between two of them. A gap as wide as a moped.

He put the ball through it. Not hard. Just enough.

Karim was already running. He took it in his stride, one touch to set it, and hit it high and hard into the top right corner. The net jumped.

Karim did not celebrate. He picked the ball out of the net, ran it back to the center circle, and put it down himself.

2-1.

On the club side the coach's hands had stopped going round. He was walking faster now, and his left leg came later every step. "Yallah," he called, and it was a different yallah.

Raja came at them. The waves again, but now Youssef could see them coming. Then Luca was through. A ball over the top, one bounce, Luca alone, nothing between him and Bilal but grass. Youssef was on the halfway line. He could not get there. He could only watch.

Luca hit it low toward the far post. A good shot.

Bilal went.

From the halfway line Youssef saw him leave the ground, the whole long length of him flat in the air above the turf, the way he had hung above the sand. Black crumbs flew up around him. His fingers found the ball and bent it, just enough, around the post.

He landed hard. The turf did not catch him the way the sand had. He lay there one second. Then he sat up, spat out a black crumb, and pointed at Chams-Eddine to take the near post.

He did not laugh this time.

The corner came in, and Chams-Eddine headed it away, high and spinning. Youssef's eyes were half-shut against the sun and missed nothing. He was not where the ball was. He was where it was going to be, already standing on the spot when it came down, as if the ground had told him first. He took it on his chest and laid it off to Younes before the white shirts had finished looking up.

Younes was by the touchline with his back to the goal and a defender breathing on his neck. The ball reached him too high, bouncing, about to go out. Anybody from the medina would have kicked it anywhere. On the stones a ball in the air is a ball you give away.

Younes did not kick it. He caught it between his heels, rolled it up the back of one calf with the other foot, and flicked it over his own head and over the defender's head, a slow arc against the blue, and it came down on the far side of the defender exactly where Younes had gone to meet it. It never touched the turf. In the bodyboard row somebody stood up and shouted his name.

Younes slid it down the line into the space in front of Isklander.

Raja's right back saw the ball. Then he looked for the red shirt, and there was only green. Isklander had been beside him and now he was past him, and the space in between held nothing anybody could see. The right back was still turning when the cross came in.

It came low and flat across the six-yard box, hard, skimming the grass.

Yazid was there. No backswing. He did not need one. He had learned to score in alleys where there was no room to swing a leg, where the wall was right there behind your heel. He put his instep to the ball and turned it, that was all, the way you turn the tap at the fountain, and it went in by the post.

2-2.

The stands came up all at once. The bodyboarders banged their boards together. Khalid held his over his head with both hands, and it was dry, and it didn't matter. In the middle of all of it Hadj Brahim leaned forward on his cane.

On the Raja bench the seven in jackets were standing.

Badr clapped his hands at his team, hard, three times. It was the first time Youssef had heard him raise his voice.

Then Yazid went down.

Nobody was near him. He was jogging back toward the halfway line, and then he was not jogging. He was on the turf with both hands around his right calf and his face pressed into the green.

Youssef got there first. The calf was a hard knot, and the knot was moving under Yazid's skin like something alive under a blanket. Yazid's teeth were together. His eyes were wet.

"It's —" Yazid said. "It's just —"

The others came. Omar. Bilal, running all the way from his goal. Karim, Isklander. They stood around him in a ring and looked down, and then they looked at each other, and then they looked at the touchline.

There was nobody on it. A row of water bottles. A plastic bag of oranges.

"If he can't —"

"Then we're ten."

"We can't play ten. Not against —"

"We —"

Nobody finished. Youssef's heart was going in his ears. Eleven. They had come with eleven. Nobody had thought about what would happen if one of them stopped. Nobody had thought Yazid would ever stop.

The Raja man with the white-cross bag was jogging onto the pitch from the club side.

But somebody was already there.

Youssef had not seen him come down from the stands. He had not seen him cross the line. He was just there, kneeling on the turf beside Yazid, in a shirt with a Y wrinkled into the back of it.

He took Yazid's heel in one hand and laid the leg out straight along the grass. He put his other hand flat against the bottom of Yazid's boot, against the tape, and leaned. Slowly. He pushed the toes back toward the shin, steady, with his weight behind it, the way Youssef's mother leaned into the dough in the morning. Not hard. Not soft. Like a man who had done it a hundred times on a field worn down to the black.

Yazid made a noise in his throat.

The knot under his skin went still. Then it let go. Youssef watched it go, the calf going soft again, like a fist opening.

The Raja man stopped two steps away with his bag. He did not kneel. He looked at the gardien's hands and nodded once, to nobody.

The referee came over, his whistle hanging on its string.

"Who are you?" he asked.

The gardien did not answer. He set Yazid's foot down on the turf the way you set down a full glass. He stood. He crossed his arms. Then he turned and walked back toward the stands, and he did not hurry, and nobody stopped him.

Yazid sat up. He pressed his calf with both thumbs. He stood, put his weight on the leg, lifted his heel, put it down.

"I'm okay," he said.

"You're sure?" Omar said.

"I'm okay." He looked at Omar. Then at Youssef. "I'm okay."

At the end of the stands the clock kept counting. It had not stopped for Yazid. It did not stop now.

Youssef looked down at his boots, at the word on them. His toes were folded tight inside, the nail gone black.

He pressed them into the turf, and the turf pressed back.
