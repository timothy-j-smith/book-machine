## 4. The senses: unpredictable

How I placed them: one sense comes in where the moment asks for it, then the story goes back to plain telling. Never two senses in a row, and no rotation. Some stretches have none at all. The kickoff, the goals and most of the second half are almost pure sight and movement. That makes the senses hit harder when they come.

- **Smell, close up, at the frozen moment.**
  - **Before:** "the way Youssef's mother leaned into the dough."
  - **Now:** "His hands knew where to go. Youssef was close enough to smell them. Coins. The metal box."
  - The time-freeze is the one place the prose gets slow enough to put the reader's nose by a stranger's hands. The coins are the ones he slid back with two fingers. No one explains it.
- **Taste, in the slump.**
  - **Before:** halftime had silence and no taste except the prickly pear image.
  - **Now:** "Youssef dug his thumb into the peel. The orange was warm from the sun and so sour his jaw ached under his ears."
  - The prickly pear is the image. The orange is the real thing in the mouth. They sit a page apart, and they're the only taste at halftime.
- **Smell in the panic, plain, no comparison.**
  - **Before:** "The turf was hot. It smelled like the tire man's shop at the bottom of the hill."
  - **Now:** "He put a hand down. Hot. Hot rubber in his nose. He was up before he knew he was up."
  - In rapids you get hit; you don't compare. Touch, then smell, then he's moving again.
- **The other senses, scattered:**
  - Sound: the radio and the baby, the hiss of the bus doors, the tape slapping the turf, and a moped "that took a long time going" inside the frozen clock.
  - Touch: the bag handles, and the heat through the soles after 2-2.
  - Taste: salt on his lip during Yazid's lines.

## How I rationed comparisons, as a teaching note

**The rule I followed:** a comparison has to bring something from Youssef's world that the plain fact can't carry. If the plain fact is already strong, the comparison is just decoration. Here are three before-and-afters:

1. **Before:** "It went from white shirt to white shirt like the tram down Boulevard Mohammed V, on rails, on time, never stopping where you were waiting."
   **After:** "White shirt to white shirt. One touch, two. Pass. One touch. Pass."
   **Why:** the rhythm of the words does what the tram did. In the panic, the boy has no time to think of a tram.
2. **Before:** "the knot was moving under Yazid's skin like something alive under a blanket."
   **After:** "The knot moved under Yazid's skin by itself, a jump, then another."
   **Why:** "by itself" is scarier than any blanket. The body is doing something the boy didn't ask for.
3. **Before:** "Eleven boys standing still in all that green, like cats caught in a moped's light at night in the alley."
   **After:** "Eleven boys standing still on all that turf. Not running. Not anything."
   **Why:** the cats are the book's myth. They can't be used for a passing joke. And "Not anything" lands harder without the picture.

**What I kept and why:** "Like something left out in the sun" for the concrete pitch stays. It's his own ground, seen from the other side of the fence for the first time, and only a comparison can make it shrink like that.

## Is the voice that came through still intact?

I think so. The sentences are still short and physical, the dialogue is still clipped, and the boy still sees more than he can explain. The goals now sound like the medina instead of a highlight reel, which makes Sparrow's chip lonelier and better. The biggest risk is the frozen-clock section: it's the slowest writing in the chapter, and it should feel like holding your breath, not like stalling. Read it out loud and tell me whether it holds or drags.
