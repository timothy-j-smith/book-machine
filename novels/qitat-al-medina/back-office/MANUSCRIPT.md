Chapter 1: Simo


The gray cat appeared at the mouth of the alley each morning before the muezzin's first call, its weight shifting from one paw to the other.

Youssef watched from the doorway of his family's apartment, barefoot, the tile cold beneath his heels. Below, the spice merchant was arranging his cones of cumin and paprika and ras el hanout, the colors already bright in the thin early light, and the cat — Simo, with his mismatched eyes, one copper and one gray — sat at the edge of the merchant's display and studied the pigeons that gathered near the fountain.

He never lunged. That was the first lesson.

Simo would sit, and the pigeons would settle, and then his weight would shift — not toward the birds but away from them, a quarter-turn that opened a line the pigeons hadn't thought to guard. When he moved, it was through the gap the birds had created by watching the wrong direction.

Youssef was eleven. He had been tracking the cats of Rue de la Douane for three years, watching them the way his father watched the evening news — elbows on knees, eyes narrowed, as if proximity to the screen might change what it was showing.

Hamza, the scarred orange tabby, kept his eyes half-closed and missed nothing. His ribs were pronounced against tight skin. He owned the plaza near the fountain and moved through the boys' football games with complete confidence. He did not flinch at the ball — he adjusted — always one step, never two, his body knowing where the ball would land before it arrived.

Lalla, the black female, kept to the doorways along Rue de la Douane and could remain perfectly still, eyes open, for over an hour and then cross three meters of open ground so fast that the motion registered only as absence — she was here, then she was there, and the space between held nothing the eye could catch.

But it was Simo, who was neither the fastest nor the strongest but who must have understood something the others did not: that the shortest distance between two points was not a line but a hesitation, a false step that opened the true path.

Over three years, their movements had entered his feet without permission. He changed direction now without planting, without telegraphing. He could feel a defender's weight shift as Hamza felt the ball's approach — not through seeing it but through the slight vibration in the air, the small disturbance that preceded the event. He could look in one direction while sending the ball in the opposite. The cobblestone had turned soft on the palms of his feet; they absorbed his landings, his jumps, his cuts. He could bend his toes around the ball, the way the cats cradled their litter.

"You move like one of them," Isklander had told him one afternoon, gesturing toward Hamza, who was watching their game from atop the fountain wall with a stillness that bordered on contempt.

In the apartment, Fatima set two glasses of mint tea on the low table and called his name. Youssef came inside and sat cross-legged on the cushion and drank. The sugar dissolving against his teeth, the steam carrying the scent into the small room where his sister Aicha still slept in the corner, her breathing even and undisturbed. Fatima brushed a piece of lint off Youssef's collar. Beyond the window, past the rooftops, the white minaret of the Hassan II Mosque rose against the Atlantic sky. No one in the family climbed the stairs to the roof to look at it.

"Your father left early," Fatima said. "Grand Stade."

Youssef nodded. His father's hands, when he came home in the evenings, carried cement dust in the creases of his knuckles and along the edges of his nails, and at dinner he held his bread carefully, as if his fingers were instruments he was still learning to use. But the men in the medina, the ones who sat outside the cafe and remembered, said that it was Ahmed Benali's feet and particularly his toes that had once done things with a football that the narrow streets still talked about. They called him The Phantom. The French papers had called him Le Fantôme, back then. Not because he was fast — others were faster — but because he was never where you expected, and by the time you understood where he'd gone, the ball was already past you.

No one talked about what happened after.

Youssef finished his tea and pulled on his Barcelona jersey — Messi on the back, the fabric faded to the color of the sky just before full dark, a gift from a French tourist who had stopped to watch a game two years before. Below, in the alley, Omar was already waiting, juggling the ball against the wall, effortlessly.

The cobblestones of Rue de la Douane were not kind. Every seam was a ridge that could send the ball skipping sideways, every drain cover bent the angle of a pass. The walls on either side stood close enough that a long pass had nowhere to go — wide enough for a moped, narrow enough that the only useful pass was a short one. The ground never forgot a rain — the stones held moisture in their joints for days, creating patches of slick ground that appeared and vanished with the weather.

The alley sloped toward the sea, toward the mosque. One team always played uphill. Ahmed taught Youssef one thing about football, only one: practice uphill.

This was the pitch. The walls were defenders. The drain covers were the bad bounces you had to read before they happened. The cats were the opponents who never committed the same way twice. And the cobblestones were the old teachers, the ones who punished a lazy touch by sending the ball into the spice merchant's display or off the wheel of a passing moped, and who rewarded a good one with nothing — the ball simply continued, and the absence of punishment was the only praise the stones offered.

Youssef stole the ball from Omar and began to move. Tap, tap, tap. He sliced the ball with his toes, creating a sharp backspin. The ball advanced a single meter into the dust, hesitated, and rolled back. He didn't look down. The ball was already at his feet, ready.

He did it again.


Chapter 2: Panenka


The phone arrived on a Tuesday. Khalid, Omar's older brother, came down the stairs holding it in both hands like a man carrying water from a well — careful, both hands.

"Watch," he said, and touched the screen, and the screen became a window.

Hakim Ziyech — The Wizard — alone at the edge of the box, the ball leaving his left foot on a line that started straight and then bent — and the net catching it, and seventy thousand people standing, and Morocco winning.

Youssef's breath left his chest.

"Again," he said.

Khalid played it again. The third time, Youssef watched not the ball but Ziyech's planted foot, the angle of his hip, the drop of his shoulder a half-second before the strike to sell the defender on a direction that didn't exist.

The 2022 World Cup was three years gone, but in the medina the memory lived in the walls. Youssef had been nine. The entire neighborhood had gathered in the Cafe La Rose De Medina, the air thick with cigarette smoke and the warm scent of harcha, cinnamon and sheeba. The room held the particular electricity of men watching something they had waited their whole lives to see.

When Hakimi chipped the penalty down the center against Spain — the goalkeeper diving left, the ball hanging in the air above the place where the goalkeeper had been — the sound that came from that room was not a cheer. It was something older. A sound that held three generations of men who had been told where they belonged and who, for ninety minutes, stood somewhere else entirely.

Morocco had finished fourth in the world.

That night Youssef and Omar had sat on the roof of Omar's building while fireworks painted the sky in red, white and green, and Youssef had said it aloud for the first time. Morocco would be one of the hosts for the 2030 World Cup. He would be seventeen. He would play.

Omar had not laughed. He had nodded once, the way a person agrees to something he will have to carry.

Now, on Khalid's phone, they found everything. Not just Morocco's games but the street videos — boys in São Paulo juggling on broken asphalt, a kid in Lagos nutmegging three defenders in a space the size of a prayer rug, teenagers in Brixton playing on a cage pitch in the rain, the ball slapping off wet concrete with a sound that carried through the phone's small speaker like a handclap.

Youssef watched their feet. He watched how the surface changed the game — the Brazilian boy rolling the ball with his sole to keep control on rough ground, the Lagos kid using the wall as a teammate, the Brixton rain making every first touch a negotiation.

"They learned the same way," Youssef said quietly.

Each evening after practice in the streets he came to Omar's apartment, and they sat with the phone between them and studied — pausing, rewinding, arguing over the placement of a foot, the timing of a pass.


Chapter 3: Vespa


His twelfth birthday began with msemen and honey, the flatbread folded and fried until the layers separated at the touch of a fork, and his father sitting across the low table in his work clothes, not yet gone, which meant he had arranged something with the foreman. The stadium could wait.

"Twelve," Ahmed said, and his hands rested on Youssef's shoulders — cement hands, the fingertips rough as dried mortar. "When I was twelve, I thought I was ready for everything."

"What happened?" Youssef asked.

His father's hands stayed on his shoulders and he looked briefly out through the small window in the corner of the kitchen. The white minaret of the Hassan II Mosque filled more of the window than the sky.

"Nothing happened. That was the problem."

Youssef did not know what to do with that. He picked at the msemen.

"Ain Diab," Youssef said. "Tomorrow. Khalid is taking us."

Ahmed's fingertips tightened — half a count — then let go. He looked back at the window.

"The water is colder than it looks," he said finally. "Even in summer."

The celebration was small. Fatima's tagine simmered. Neighbors in the courtyard. Aicha handed Youssef a crown made from cardboard and bottle caps that cast small bright circles on the walls. Aicha's eyes were Fatima's eyes, the same black.

In the shadows, Old Hadj Brahim, who had known Ahmed since they were boys, leaned in close. "The Phantom," he said. "Sent a ball through a gap between two parked cars. Barely wider than the ball itself. And it came back to his foot. Like it knew the way home."

Youssef listened. The story was a gift. It was also a weight.

The next morning, Youssef climbed onto the back of Khalid's blue Vespa behind Omar, and the three of them rode west out of the crowded medina, skimming past side mirrors, past the elbows of pedestrians, cats darting between moped wheels. Youssef stared up at the sky as the road bent toward the coast and onto Boulevard Sidi Mohammed Ben Abdallah.

The Atlantic first arrived not through his eyes but through his lungs — the salt hit the back of his throat before the blue hit his vision, his ribs expanded. And there it stood. The Hassan II Mosque. He had not been here since he was little. The memory was thin. The minaret rose two hundred and ten meters above him, a finger of bone pointing at the sky. Khalid and Omar continued talking about body surfing and football and jumping the waves, but Youssef could not hear.

His neck tilted back. The mosque listened.

Place Ain Diab. The beach. Families under umbrellas, the bright fabric snapping in the offshore wind. A row of camels resting near the boardwalk, their saddles bright with green and red wool, an old man in a black djellaba taking children for rides at twenty dirhams. Further down, horses for rent — slow, salt-stained, their riders too tall for them. Vendors selling fried sardines from steel carts, the oil hissing in the offshore wind. Children running at the edge of the surf, their shouts mixed with the call of gulls. And further down still, where the sand was packed hard enough to hold weight, a group of boys kicking a ball, no shoes, no shirts, no whistles, no rules.

Youssef stepped onto the sand and the ground changed its language.

Every first touch was softer. Every stride shorter. The ball didn't skip or argue — it stuck, and the sand absorbed the energy of his passes and returned something slower, something that had to be worked for. His calves burned in a place they had never burned before, deep in the muscles that governed balance, and for the first few minutes his body was a stranger to itself — the feet that knew cobblestones intimately did not yet know this ground.

But the sand forgave. He fell and the sand caught him. He reached for a ball and the ground received him. He tried a bicycle kick, and when he landed on his back the breath left him and then came back, the sand holding the shape of his body.

Karim was taller than the other coastal boys, thin as rope, and he moved through the sand without appearing to touch it. He played with the ocean's rhythm — no pause, each action flowing into the next without the stops and starts that the medina game demanded. Beside Karim was Younes — lean, dark-skinned, his curls bleached by sun and salt — the ball balanced on his foot like a thing he had practiced alone. He watched Karim's grace. He watched Youssef's hunger.

"You're from the old city," Karim said.

"How do you know?" Youssef replied.

"Your feet. Your movement, it's the cobblestones," Karim said.

Youssef looked down at his feet, bare, the soles cracked and hard from years of stone. "Maybe," he said.

"Out here the sand forgives. That's why we play different."

They played until the orange sun hung low over the water, and the game merged — medina precision passing into coastal flow, Youssef's tight turns opening into longer strides, Karim's sweeping runs folding into smaller spaces. Younes ran between them, finding the spaces neither of them had yet claimed. When the game ended, Omar and Khalid ran for the surf, salt already in their hair, the medina two hours behind them.

"There's a concrete pitch near here," Karim said when they stopped. "Real goals. You should come back. Bring your medina."


Chapter 4: Jellies


The concrete pitch sat to the side of a chain-link fence in a public park, its surface cracked and bleached, the white painted lines faded to a grayish bone color. A tall chain-link fence ran through the center of the park, separating the public park from the private facility. Complexe Sportif Al Amal. Raja Club Athletic's football pitches were perfect. Everything about them was right angles, symmetry, and crispness — like the incoming breeze from the shore. Artificial turf the color of money, white lines sharp as ruled paper, goals with nets that hung without a wrinkle.

Youssef played on the concrete.

He had brought Isklander, whose speed on the medina's narrow streets translated into something startling on open ground, and Chams-Eddine, who read the shape of a play as Simo read the pigeons — watching the spaces between the players, not the players themselves. Yazid — a boy built low and wide through the shoulders — had come because he went where there were competitions. His internal engine stronger than the Vespa's.

Karim brought his coastal boys, Younes at his side, all in blue jellies and white socks. The two groups met on the cracked concrete, and within ten minutes the football was spontaneous enough to stop people. A woman carrying grocery bags set them down. Two old men on a bench turned to watch. A teenager recording on his phone held the screen steady and did not look away.

The sequence was sudden: ball to Omar, Omar's weighted pass splitting two defenders, Youssef collecting with the outside of his right foot and turning in the same motion. By the time the ball settled he was already facing the goal, the first defender behind him, the second arriving after the ball was gone. The finish was low and certain in the far corner. Only then did the rhythmic sound begin.

"Qit sihri." Quiet at first, from one person in the crowd, and then another. "Qit sihri. Qit sihri."

Magic cat.

The academy boys appeared behind their fence the way people appear at an accident — drawn by the event, careful to maintain distance. Their Adidas tracksuits matched. Their boots were clean.

"Not bad," Luca said, loud enough to carry. He was short and sturdy, his confidence ahead of him. "For jellies." He punched a teammate in the arm and laughed.

Youssef walked toward the fence, the concrete warm underneath his bare soles, and spoke.

"I'm going to play for Morocco. In the World Cup."

The silence was brief. Then the laughter — not cruel exactly, but carrying something harder than amusement. Badr, the academy captain, tall and unhurried, looked at Youssef's bare feet, his faded jersey, the cracked concrete and the coastal boys' jellies.

"The World Cup requires proper training," Badr said. "Coaches. Facilities." He gestured toward the concrete. "Not that."

Youssef thought for a moment. "Then let's play," he said.

Badr studied him as a person studies a math problem that has produced an unexpected answer.

"Our pitch. Two Fridays from now," Badr said.

Luca laughed. "But when you lose, you all stop pretending. Back to the beach. Back to the cobblestones."

Youssef felt Omar's firm hand on his back, felt Karim's breath at his ear. Through the sudden quiet, one of the old men on the bench — the smaller one, his cheeks weathered like a fisherman's, his hands resting on a cane between his knees — kept the chanting alive, his voice low and steady against the concrete pitch.


Chapter 5: Weight


They did not own boots.

The first week was a scramble — borrowing, begging, trading favors. One pair of boots arrived from the French tourist, who appeared at the spice merchant's door with a Nike box. The box was a reddish-orange with the words PHANTOM ELITE.

"Pour le chat magique," he said.

The boots were too small. Youssef squeezed his toes into them. The cobblestones felt different now, solid underneath shoes.

They practiced on a small balding artificial turf in the heart of Hay Mohammadi, just off the busy lanes of Boulevard Ali Yaata. A neighborhood turf — Terrain de Proximité. The apartment buildings around it stood five stories high, their facades a faded white that had once been brighter, their windows latticed with metal bars, the laundry on the balconies their only flag.

The gardien was there. He was always there. When he crossed his arms, the back of his shirt wrinkled into a perfect Y. He didn't say much. He picked up his clipboard and nodded. He understood.

The surface was alien — flat, consistent, the ball running true and fast, in a way that felt dishonest after the cobblestones' constant argument and the beach's forgiveness. Passes arrived exactly where you aimed them. First touches didn't negotiate. The ground had no opinion.

"It's too easy," Isklander said and then overhit a pass that skipped off the turf at twice the speed he'd intended and struck the surrounding wall, green and white paint still wet: F'BLADI DELMOUNI.

"Not easy," Youssef said. "Different. Like the sand was different."

They learned. The way the turf pushed back against a planted foot. The way the ball skidded rather than rolled. The way a sprint on artificial grass felt like running on a drumskin — responsive, fast, the energy returning through the soles. Omar drew formations on a flattened cardboard box and propped it against the wall. They walked the patterns, then ran them, then played them at speed until the patterns dissolved into instinct and the instinct became movement. While the others drank from water bottles, Youssef licked the salt from his upper lip and watched Yazid run the lines. End to end. The boy never quit. Youssef glanced down at his boot — PHANTOM ELITE prominent again — he ran.

The gardien unfolded his arms and wiped his face with the back of his wrist. The corner of his mouth turned slightly upward toward the sun.

Two Fridays arrived hot and bright, the sky a blue so deep it looked solid. Word came around that Raja Club Athlétique had moved the match to their premier pitch, complete with an official referee, linesmen, a new clock and scoreboard.

The pitch was a stadium in miniature — bleachers, floodlights, nets white and taut. Raja arrived in a team bus. Their warm-up was choreographed, each player moving through the same sequence at the same time, their touches crisp and identical. Perfect.

The coach was a slight man, salt-and-pepper hair, green-and-white tracksuit. He walked the touchline with a limp he had earned long ago and long since forgiven. His hands circled in front of him. "Yallah, yallah," he called.

Youssef's team arrived on scooters and bicycles and a city bus that dropped off teammates, family, and friends from the medina. Their jerseys were counterfeit Morocco kits that had appeared through channels no one could name. Chams-Eddine's boots were two different sizes. Yazid had wrapped black electrical tape around his right shoe where the sole was separating from the upper. None of their shorts or socks matched.

The crowd surprised both sides. The story had traveled — medina and beach against the academy, cobblestones and sand versus artificial turf. Moroccan U14 National Champions versus an unknown. Families came. Old men who had once played on streets like the ones Youssef played on came. Teenagers with cell phones raised came. Another group showed up with surfboards in hand. It was Friday evening, and the crowd attracted more of a crowd.

Badr shook Youssef's hand at the center circle. His eyes went to Youssef's jammed toes.

"Ready?" Badr asked.

"Yes," Youssef replied.

Raja played with machine precision — each part in service to the next, each movement drilled and calibrated. Their passes ran on rails. Their pressing came in coordinated waves. The turf was theirs; they knew its speed, its bounce, the way the ball sat up or skidded. Their coach stood at the touchline, vocal and loud, pointing instructions with his index finger.

Youssef's team spent ten minutes chasing shadows.

The first goal came from a corner — Badr rising above Chams-Eddine, the header traveling a clean line into the back of the net. The second came five minutes before halftime, a loose pass from Omar intercepted and converted with two touches — trap, finish, the ball in the net before Bilal set his feet.

2-0.

Under the bleachers, in the thin shade, the silence sat in their mouths like a prickly pear — the small spines they had not seen, the sweet flesh further in than they could reach without bleeding. Chams-Eddine sat with his head between his knees. Isklander stared at his borrowed boots. The medina boys and the coastal boys sat apart, cobblestone and sand.

Youssef said nothing for a long time. He was thinking about Simo — about the gray cat sitting at the edge of the spice merchant's display, watching the pigeons settle, waiting for the gap that their comfort would create.

"We've been playing their game," he said finally. The words were quiet, directed at the ground. "We don't play their game." Youssef paused. "We play our mix."

He looked at Karim. Karim was already nodding.

The second half began, and the medina and the beach arrived together as one. Not the football they had tried to play in the first forty-five — careful, imitative, respectable — but the real thing. A blind exchange between Youssef and Omar at the touchline, the way they did in the medina. A long coastal stride from Karim folding into the medina's tight rotation as though it had always belonged.

Youssef received the ball in his own half with three defenders between him and the goal. His first touch was a nutmeg — the ball played to himself through the midfielder's legs, a move so casual it looked accidental. But his body was already turning as the ball went through, and by the time the defender understood, Youssef was two meters past him. The second defender committed to a challenge; Youssef dropped his shoulder left and went right, the ball glued to his instep. The third was a pass — weighted, angled, threaded through a gap that opened for a half-second as the defensive line shifted. Karim ran onto it, his finish clean, hard, into the upper right corner.

There was no celebration. 2-1.

The equalizer came ten minutes later. Isklander's speed, which in the medina's narrow streets was also a tool of survival, on this wide pitch was something the academy's right back had never seen — a motion that registered only as absence, the way Lalla crossed three meters of open ground. The cross was low and flat. Yazid met it with his instep — not a spectacular strike, but a precise one, the ball redirected into the net. Someone who had learned to score within spaces where there is no room for a backswing.

2-2.

The crowd was on its feet like the Derby. Old Hadj Brahim leaned forward on his cane and did not blink. The teenager's phone was steady. The two old men on the bench were on their feet. The scoreboard had become a question.


Chapter 6: Sparrow


Four minutes remained.

Raja pressed forward, but their pressing had changed — it was hurried, reactive, and hurry creates space. Youssef collected a loose ball just inside his own half. He had felt the bounce before the bounce arrived — the way Hamza on the fountain wall had always felt the ball coming. Thirty plus meters from his own goal. Sixty from the other. The pitch opened ahead of him the way the medina never did — wide, green, without walls.

He ran. His cramped toes felt free again.

The first midfielder came at an angle and Youssef shifted the ball to his left foot without breaking stride, the way you step around a puddle, the defender's challenge arriving at the place where the ball had been a half-second before. The second defender dropped into the passing lane, but Youssef wasn't looking for a pass. He was looking at the space — the column of green between the center-back and the right back, a gap that would exist for two seconds at most before the defensive line corrected.

He pushed the ball forward with the inside of his right foot, a touch that sent it a few meters ahead and into the gap, and then he ran — through the green the way he ran through the medina when a game spilled from an alley into the wider street, the way Lalla moved when she decided to cross the open ground — and the ball was there when he arrived and the center-back was not.

The goalkeeper came off his line. He was tall and wore all black. He made himself large the way goalkeepers are taught.

Youssef lifted the ball.

Not struck — lifted. The way his mother placed bread on the cloth at dinner. A touch so soft it seemed to cost nothing, and the ball rose over the goalkeeper's outstretched gloves and fell into the net, the net moving the way a curtain moves when a window is opened — gentle, barely disturbed.

The sound that followed was the sound the Cafe La Rose De Medina made when Hakimi's penalty floated down the center three years before.

And then the flag went up. Offside. The referee walked over to talk with the linesman. The call stood.

Raja snatched the ball from the back of the net and set it down for the free-kick. The goalkeeper drove a long ball to Badr on the left flank. Badr trapped it cleanly, taking three quick, aggressive touches past midfield. The medina boys tracked back. Badr lifted his head and lofted the ball to the far post and Luca took it on his chest, dropped it to his foot, and drilled it low and hard. Bilal dove as if reaching across sand. His outstretched glove grazed nothing.

3-2.

The Raja faithful cheered. The quietness was louder.

There would be no extra time and the last minute wound down quickly, the medina pressing for the equalizer. The final seconds dissolved with the sounds of boots on turf.

Then the evening call to prayer rose from the mosque beyond the park. The muezzin's voice drifted over the floodlights, over the chain-link fence, settling across both pitches — the cracked concrete and the green turf — reaching all the way to Stade Mohammed V without distinguishing between them.

The final whistle came.

The medina boys gathered in a small ragged circle. Omar's hand stayed around Youssef's neck, pulling him close. Karim and the coastal boys had traded their cleats for jellies and white socks, soft shapes printing in the turf. Youssef had pulled the boots off. The turf burned, and he stood on it anyway.

In the bleachers, Hadj Brahim lifted his cane. They had lost, and the families applauded anyway.

The Raja players moved toward the green and white bus, talking among themselves, not looking back. Luca raised his arm and waved it like a flag — the offside call, again. He turned to Badr. Badr had not moved. Luca held there a moment, then ran to catch the others.

Badr found Youssef.

"That goal," Badr said, looking down at his own feet.

"Didn't count," Youssef replied.

"It counted," Badr said. "It counted," he repeated.

"I know," Youssef said. He smiled.

"Coach wanted to know." Badr paused. He looked at Youssef's bare feet, the PHANTOM ELITE boots tied together and hanging from his fingers. "Where did you learn?"

"Rue de la Douane," Youssef said. He looked back toward the city. "Qitat al-Medina."

Badr did not answer immediately.

"Your father?" Badr asked.

"Benali," Youssef replied.

Badr glanced toward the touchline where the Coach had stood all afternoon. "Akesbi. He played with him."

Youssef looked up.

"The Sparrow," Badr said.

"The Sparrow," Youssef repeated.

"He knows you now."

Badr held the look for one breath longer than expected. Then he turned toward the green and white academy bus.


Chapter 7: Phantom


The ride home was all wind and the Vespa's engine rising in pitch and echoes as the streets narrowed around them. Youssef rode on the back. In the medina, the story was traveling — and something pressed against his ribs.

He climbed the stairs to the apartment. The kitchen was dim. In the back room, his father sat on the low bench, the cedar chest open before him, something red folded across his knees. The gold of an embroidered emblem caught the lamplight. Ahmed folded the cloth over it.

He looked up, saw his son in the doorway, closed the chest, and they walked out to the courtyard together.

His face was darkened by the sun. Something in his eyes had opened.

"They say you scored from thirty meters," Ahmed said.

"Maybe twenty-five," Youssef replied.

"They say the goalkeeper never touched it."

Youssef nodded. "The Phantom."

Ahmed reached. His hand stopped, the lime-stained fingers hanging an inch from cotton. Then it settled on Youssef's shoulder. The weight was different from the weight at the birthday table. Not heavier. Rearranged.

Youssef's ribs loosened.

"They broke ground on the mosque the year I was born." Ahmed tilted his head toward the sea. "I was seven when they set the last pillar." A pause. "That's what they called it then. Great Hope Watching Over Us."

Youssef nodded.

"I still see that last pillar going up." Ahmed closed his eyes. "But we cracked."

He opened his eyes. He looked at his son. Youssef tried to speak. Nothing came.

His father's shoulders had rounded over the years and Youssef hadn't noticed until now.

They both looked out through the narrow gap between buildings where the evening light fell in the medina — not directly but reflected off the opposite wall, arriving warm and secondhand, carrying the smell of cumin and cinnamon from the merchant's shop below.

In the echoes, Youssef could hear a ball on cobblestones. Other boys. Younger perhaps.

He held his father's hand for the first time in many years.

Above them, someone's laundry flapped in the night breeze. Beneath, red and white graffiti faded into the brick: MI ALMA VA A GANAR. Together they stood, fingers entangled, and listened to the sounds the streets made.

Below, in the doorway of the spice merchant's shop, Simo sat with his mismatched eyes half-closed, watching the younger boys play, his weight shifting from one paw to the other. Patient. Still. Reading the angles.

And into the night, the game went on.
