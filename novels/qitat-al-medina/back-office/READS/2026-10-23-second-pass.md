## 1. What the notes changed in my reading

**"The Phantom" in Chapter 7.** Earlier tonight I said the Bible's "A Phantom" meant Youssef was naming himself. The notes show the opposite. The change to "The Phantom" was your deliberate final move: the son naming his father out loud for the first time in twenty-two years. That's the moment the story is reaching for, and it's a fine instinct. But the page doesn't yet set it up as a first. Chapter 1 has the men outside the café calling Ahmed "The Phantom" openly, so a reader doesn't know the name is never said at home. For the line to land as a first, the page needs one earlier beat where the name is avoided in the apartment: someone starts to say it and stops, or Fatima changes the subject. Then "The Phantom" in Chapter 7 breaks a silence the reader has watched being kept.

**Ahmed.** He's a medina boy who came up through Raja's academy, an elite national-team player, and part of the 2010 squad. He was frozen out afterward, and now he pours concrete for the stadium that will host the World Cup he never reached. This is far richer than what's on the page, and I'll use it below.

**The mosque.** Covenant and witness. It's the institution that should have held them and instead keeps the ledger of their failure. The family hasn't walked its esplanade in twenty-two years.

**The offside.** The notes show the bad call was meant to be honored with *"The Raja faithful cheered. The quietness was louder."* Your wall-pass fix still stands.

### The five failure modes

**1. Too neat. Partly agree.** You say the bad call is the surprise. It's a real turn, but a reader trained on sports stories half-expects a late reversal. What lifts it above formula is Badr's *"It counted,"* not the flag itself. The fix isn't a different result. It's one beat elsewhere that the reader truly doesn't see coming (see point 4).

I found it in the arc: Youssef never doubts, never fails personally, and doesn't change. Both come from the same root. Youssef is an idealized boy. He needs one moment where he's wrong, petty or afraid, and it should cost something.

**3. Closing catalogue.** Fixed.

**4. No surprise beat. Agree.** You proposed Badr stomping on Youssef's toes, or saying *"Don't come around here anymore."* **I disagree with that one.** It would break Badr, who is the book's best-built secondary character. His *"It counted"* only works because he's honest. Cruelty from him would be a twist without a reason. The better surprise is the one already sitting in your research.

**My candidate: Ahmed was at the match.** In Chapter 7 he says *"They say you scored from thirty meters."* That sounds like a man repeating gossip. Suppose instead he knows something only a witness could know: which part of the foot, or that Youssef looked at the linesman before he looked at the net. The reader and Youssef realize at the same moment that he was there. Where? On the public side of the chain-link fence, on the cracked concrete, because a man frozen out of the elite clubs doesn't walk into Raja's ground. That uses your real-world origin image (the fence), your research (the banishment), and the empty middle of the father arc. It costs no new scene.

Professor K's other candidates are good small turns: the gardien speaks once, the old man on the bench is someone specific, or Aicha says one thing nobody expects. One of them could also be Akesbi: he played with Ahmed, so perhaps he's the man on the bench at the concrete pitch in Chapter 4, scouting, before we know who he is.

**5. Flat emotional register. Agree, and this meets the cold read's offside note.** I said nobody on Youssef's side protests the call. Professor K says the register never drops into real anger or grief. It's the same moment, seen two ways. When the flag goes up, Youssef needs to break: shout at the linesman, kick the ball away, or say something he'll be ashamed of later. That makes the corruption visible, drops the register, and gives the idealized boy his first flaw. Then *"The quietness was louder"* lands harder, because we've just heard noise.

Professor K's lesson 5, on rendering Ahmed rough, is partly done. The hand stopping an inch from the shoulder is in. *"That's what they called it then. Great Hope Watching Over Us"* still sounds like a man reading a plaque. See section 4.

## 4. The research: what's usable, and what to check

### What's usable

The 2010 campaign gives *"we cracked"* exactly what it needed. A squad that finished last in its group, in the year Africa hosted its first World Cup, followed by federation chaos and players frozen out: that's a public humiliation a family would never speak of. Combined with a 1986 birth and Ahmed pouring concrete at the Grand Stade, the timeline holds together, and it matches what I recommended earlier tonight.

### Treat with care

Much of the research reads like a search summary. The citation markers ([1], [2]) have no sources attached. Some claims I can confirm, and some I can't. These look shaky, or have consequences:

- **Akesbi.** Hassan Akesbi is, as far as I know, a real Moroccan football legend: a striker who starred in the French league in the 1950s and 60s. Moroccan readers will hear that name as a famous family, not as an invented coach. That may be fine or even useful, but it should be a choice.
- **"El-Zouffri / El-Monshidi" for The Sparrow.** As far as I know, the Arabic for sparrow is *asfour*, and *zoufri* is Darija slang with a quite different meaning. Have someone who speaks Darija check before using any of these.
- **The real Phantom and Sparrow** (El Hadrioui and Bassir). I can't verify these nicknames. If they're real, your fictional Phantom shares a name with a real 1990s player. That's worth knowing.
- **"Great Hope Watching Over Us."** I don't know this as an established name for the mosque. If it's invented, Ahmed's *"That's what they called it then"* claims a history that isn't real. If it's real, find the Darija or French form a man of 39 would actually use. Either way, it's the line in Chapter 7 that sounds most like a plaque.
- **The 2010 results.** Check the claim of no wins and three goals in six games, and the four coaches. Above all, check where the decisive Cameroon match was played. My memory is that the last group match against Cameroon was played in Morocco, at Fès, not in Yaoundé. If so, the Ahmed prose's *"final, suffocating match in Yaoundé"* needs changing, or it stays as fiction knowingly. I'm not certain of this; verify it.

### Leftovers from the old 1993 timeline in the Ahmed prose

These need reconciling if any of it moves into the book:

- *"the boy born in 1993."*
- *"Yaoundé thirty years ago."*
- *"the Cameroonian stud had caught him in 2014."*
- *"twenty-one years old."*
- Rachid's *"in the café in '93."*
- *"twenty-two years"* versus *"twenty years ago."* Under the 1986 timeline, 2010 to 2025 is fifteen years, not twenty-two.

## 6. Point of view

Good news first: across the seven chapters, true head-hopping is rare. You don't jump into another character's thoughts for paragraphs at a time. What you have is a handful of small slips, plus a separate problem that feels like head-hopping but isn't.

### Small slips (Youssef couldn't know these)

- **Chapter 3.** *"He watched Karim's grace. He watched Youssef's hunger."* "Hunger" is Younes's reading of Youssef. One fix: *"His eyes went from Karim to Youssef and stayed there."*
- **Chapter 5.** The French tourist *"had heard about the match through the medina's nervous system of gossip."* This one is mild; Youssef could guess it.
- **Chapter 5.** The gardien *"understood."* Understood what? This is borderline. If it's Youssef's guess, give it to him: *"Youssef thought he understood."* Or cut it, because the nod already carries it.
- **Chapter 5.** The coach's limp, *"long since forgiven."* Youssef can see a limp but not a forgiveness.
- **Chapter 5.** *"By the time the defender understood, Youssef was two meters past him."* Sports writing does this all the time, but it's the defender's mind. *"By the time the defender turned"* stays outside him.
- **Chapter 5.** Isklander's speed was *"something the academy's right back had never seen."* That's the right back's experience.

### The bigger issue: distance, not head-hopping

Some passages leave Youssef's head without entering anyone else's. They rise into an older, wiser narrator:

- **Chapter 1.** *"This was the pitch. The walls were defenders…"*
- **Chapter 2.** *"A sound that held three generations of men who had been told where they belonged…"*
- **Chapter 5.** *"Someone who had learned to score within spaces where there is no room for a backswing."*

This is what Professor K called Youssef being too mature. It isn't wrong. Close third can breathe out like this. But it's where the telling creeps back in, and it's where the narrator's voice drowns out the boy's.

**The test:** could an eleven-year-old from Rue de la Douane think this sentence in his own words? If not, either the narrator is allowed to rise above him (a choice you make deliberately, sparingly), or the sentence should be cut.

### On changing point of view

The Ahmed prose is evidence that the book wants Ahmed's inner life. **My view:** keep the point of view on Youssef and bring Ahmed's best moments in as things Youssef witnesses, not things Ahmed thinks. Here's how the Ahmed prose can be harvested without leaving Youssef:

- **The ball.** A ball rolls to Ahmed's work boot, and he picks it up with his hand. This works in the medina alley, with Youssef watching, as easily as at Benslimane. It's the father arc's missing middle in one image, and it's a surprise to the reader.
- **The chest.** Youssef is awake behind the curtain while Ahmed opens the chest and Fatima crosses the room and pins his hand. He sees the color and the detail, and he doesn't understand them yet. This gives Fatima her weight and plants the jersey.
- **The Grand Stade.** Youssef brings his father lunch at Benslimane. He sees the bowl his father is building for 2030. He sees the young laborers' game. He may see Rachid's thumb on the scar. He sees his father not play.

Each of these keeps the mystery that makes Ahmed powerful while finally putting the wound on stage. Robinson's *Gilead* keeps the father's voice. *The Body* keeps the boy's eye. In your book the father's silence is the subject, so the boy's eye serves it better.
